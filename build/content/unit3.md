# Unit 3 - Training Needs Assessment, Training Design, Delivery, Methods and Evaluation

This unit follows the complete training cycle: identifying needs (TNA at organisation, task and person level), designing the programme (objectives, content, methods, trainers, budget), delivering it effectively through on-the-job, off-the-job and e-learning methods, and evaluating it with the Kirkpatrick model.

> [key] Formulas and models to remember: **Training need = Standard (expected) performance - Actual performance**; the **four-stage training cycle** (TNA - planning/design - implementation - evaluation); **Kirkpatrick's four levels** (Reaction - Learning - Behaviour - Results); **Noe's definition** of training as a planned effort by the company to facilitate learning of job-related competencies.

## Q41 | 8 | Explain the concept of Training Needs Assessment (TNA) and state its importance in HRD.

### 1. Meaning of Training Needs Assessment

**Training Needs Assessment (TNA)** - also called training needs analysis - is the systematic process of identifying the gap between the performance or competence that an organisation requires and the performance or competence that its employees currently possess, and of deciding whether and how training can close that gap. It is the **first stage of the training cycle** and the foundation on which design, delivery and evaluation rest.

The basic formula is:

```
      Training need  =  Standard (expected) performance  -  Actual performance
```

A need exists when something that should be happening is not happening, or something that should not be happening is happening. However, not every performance gap is a training need - gaps caused by poor tools, unclear goals, lack of motivation or faulty systems require non-training solutions. TNA therefore also separates training problems from non-training problems.

### 2. Levels of TNA

| Level | Question asked | Sources of information |
|---|---|---|
| Organisation analysis | Where in the organisation is training needed, and does it fit the strategy, resources and climate? | Business goals and plans, performance indicators, quality and customer data, turnover, HRD climate, management support |
| Task (operational) analysis | What knowledge, skills and attitudes (KSAs) are required to perform each job well? | Job descriptions, task inventories, competency frameworks, observation, expert interviews, standards |
| Person (individual) analysis | Who needs training and in what? What is the gap between required and actual competence for each person? | Performance appraisal, tests, self-assessment, supervisor ratings, 360-degree feedback, critical incidents, interviews |

### 3. Steps in conducting a TNA

1. Clarify the performance problem or opportunity and link it to organisational goals.
2. Define the required standard of performance and the competencies behind it (task analysis).
3. Measure actual performance and competence (person analysis).
4. Identify the gap and analyse its causes - skill, knowledge, attitude, motivation, resources or system.
5. Decide which gaps can be closed by training and which need other interventions.
6. Prioritise needs by business impact, urgency and number of people affected.
7. Convert priority needs into training objectives and pass them to the design stage.

### 4. Methods of collecting data

Questionnaires and surveys, interviews with employees and managers, observation of work, performance and quality records, appraisal and potential appraisal data, tests and assessment centres, focus groups, customer feedback, and analysis of accidents, errors and complaints.

### 5. Importance of TNA in HRD

1. **Ensures relevance** - training addresses real gaps that matter to the organisation, not popular topics.
2. **Links training to strategy** - organisation analysis connects programmes to business goals, which is the first principle of HRD system design.
3. **Prevents waste of resources** - money and time are spent only where training can make a difference; needs that are really system or motivation problems are handled correctly.
4. **Identifies the right participants** - people are selected on genuine need rather than as a reward or a routine, avoiding the "fringe benefit" problem found in many Indian organisations.
5. **Provides the basis for objectives and design** - clear needs produce clear learning objectives, appropriate content and suitable methods.
6. **Makes evaluation possible** - the baseline of actual performance measured in TNA is what training effectiveness is later compared against.
7. **Builds employee readiness and commitment** - involving employees in identifying needs increases motivation to learn.
8. **Strengthens the HRD system** - TNA draws on appraisal, counselling and potential appraisal and feeds career and succession planning, integrating training with the other subsystems.
9. **Supports change** - it reveals the capabilities the organisation must build before new technology, processes or strategies are introduced.

### 6. Consequences of skipping TNA

Irrelevant programmes, wrong participants, training for problems that training cannot solve, low transfer of learning, no baseline for evaluation and loss of credibility for the HRD function. TNA is often skipped because it is seen as time-consuming and costly or because managers do not want to face the real causes of problems - but the cost of skipping it is far higher.

### 7. Conclusion

Training Needs Assessment is the diagnostic stage of training: it identifies the gap between required and actual performance at organisation, task and person levels, determines whether training is the right remedy and sets priorities and objectives. Its importance lies in making training relevant, strategic, economical, targeted and measurable - in short, in ensuring that training develops people in ways that the organisation actually needs.

## Q42 | 8 | Describe the major steps involved in designing a training program.

### 1. Introduction

Designing a training programme is the second stage of the training cycle, after Training Needs Assessment and before delivery and evaluation. Design converts an identified need into a concrete plan: what participants will learn, how, from whom, where, when and at what cost. A training plan describes the key decisions, tasks and resources needed to carry out the training. The prescribed notes summarise the components of a training plan as **training goal, learning objectives, learning methods and activities, facilities and resource persons, and budget**. The major steps are described below.

### 2. Steps in designing a training programme

#### Step 1 - Confirm the needs and define the training goal

Review the TNA findings: who needs training, in what, and why it matters to the organisation. State the overall **training goal** - the result or capability to be achieved by implementing the plan (for example, "reduce billing errors in the accounts department by 50 per cent").

#### Step 2 - Write specific learning objectives

Convert the goal into **learning objectives** - statements of what participants will be able to do as a result of the training, under what conditions and to what standard. Objectives should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. Example: "After training, the participant will be able to process a customer return correctly within three minutes without supervisor help." Objectives guide content, methods and evaluation.

#### Step 3 - Analyse the trainees

Consider the number of participants, their existing knowledge, education, experience, learning styles, language, motivation and readiness. Adult learners need relevance, participation, respect for experience and immediate applicability.

#### Step 4 - Decide the content and sequence

Select the knowledge, skills and attitudes to be covered, based on the task analysis. Organise content into modules in a logical sequence - simple to complex, known to unknown, theory followed by practice. Remove anything that does not serve an objective.

#### Step 5 - Choose the training methods and learning activities

Match methods to objectives: lectures and e-learning for knowledge; demonstration, practice, simulation and on-the-job coaching for skills; case studies, role plays, group discussion and games for attitudes and decision making. Decide the key questions raised in the notes: on the job or off the job? in-house or outsourced? on-site or off-site? which mix of methods?

#### Step 6 - Develop or procure learning materials

Prepare lesson plans, trainer guides, participant handouts, presentations, videos, exercises, cases, job aids and e-learning modules - or buy them from vendors if that is more economical.

#### Step 7 - Select the trainers and resource persons

Choose internal experts (cost-effective, know the context) or external trainers (specialised expertise) based on the subject, availability and credibility. Brief them on objectives and participants; prepare internal trainers through train-the-trainer sessions.

#### Step 8 - Arrange facilities, schedule and logistics

Decide venue (classroom, workplace, off-site, virtual), audio-visual equipment, seating, materials, meals, travel and accommodation. Schedule sessions considering shift patterns, workload, length of sessions and breaks, and spacing for practice between modules.

#### Step 9 - Prepare the budget

Itemise trainer fees, materials, venue, equipment, travel, participants' time and administrative costs so that the plan complies with the organisation's accounting procedures and can be compared with expected benefits.

#### Step 10 - Plan for transfer of learning and evaluation

Build in pre-training briefing by supervisors, post-training action plans, follow-up coaching and job aids. Decide in advance how the programme will be evaluated at the Kirkpatrick levels - reaction forms, tests, on-the-job observation, performance data - and pilot-test the design if possible.

### 3. The training plan at a glance

| Component | Content of the plan |
|---|---|
| Training goal | Overall result or capability to be achieved |
| Learning objectives | What participants will be able to do after the training |
| Learning methods and activities | Lectures, workshops, case studies, simulations, on-the-job practice, e-learning |
| Facilities and resource persons | Venue, audio-visual aids, materials, meals, travel, choice of trainers |
| Budget | Specific costs, stated to comply with accounting procedures |
| Evaluation plan | Measures and timing for reaction, learning, behaviour and results |

### 4. Conclusion

Designing a training programme moves systematically from the confirmed need to the goal, from the goal to measurable learning objectives, and from objectives to content, methods, materials, trainers, logistics, budget and an evaluation plan. A well-designed programme is one in which every element can be traced back to an objective and every objective can be traced back to a need.

## Q43 | 8 | Explain on-the-job and off-the-job training methods with suitable examples.

### 1. Introduction

Training methods are broadly classified according to **where** learning takes place. **On-the-job training (OJT)** happens at the actual workplace while the employee performs the job, usually under the guidance of a supervisor or experienced colleague. **Off-the-job training** takes place away from the normal work situation - in a classroom, training centre, vestibule or online - so that the learner can concentrate on learning without the pressure of production.

### 2. On-the-job training methods

| Method | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Job instruction training (JIT) | Step-by-step coaching: prepare the learner, present the task, let the learner try, follow up | A new machine operator is shown each step, performs it under observation and is corrected on the spot |
| Coaching | Regular guidance and feedback from the immediate superior on actual work | A sales manager accompanies a new salesperson on customer calls and reviews each call afterwards |
| Mentoring | A senior person guides a junior's overall development, career and organisational understanding | A general manager mentors a management trainee over two years |
| Job rotation | Planned movement through different jobs or departments to build broad skills | A bank probationer spends six months each in deposits, credit, foreign exchange and operations |
| Apprenticeship | Long-term structured training combining work and instruction for skilled trades | Electricians, fitters and machinists trained under the Apprentices Act |
| Understudy / assistant-to positions | The learner works as assistant to a senior and gradually takes over responsibilities | A deputy plant manager prepared to succeed the plant manager |
| Committee assignments and special projects | Participation in task forces or projects that require new skills | A young engineer leads a cost-reduction project team |
| Internship | Students or fresh recruits gain supervised practical experience | Management interns working on live business problems |

**Advantages:** learning is realistic and directly relevant; no separate facilities are needed; transfer of learning is immediate; employees remain productive; it is economical; the learner receives instant feedback.

**Limitations:** quality depends on the skill and willingness of the supervisor; bad habits may be passed on; production pressure may cut short the training; errors during learning can damage equipment, materials or customer relations; it is unsuitable for teaching theory or for large groups at once.

### 3. Off-the-job training methods

| Method | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Lectures and classroom instruction | Oral presentation of information to a group, often with visual aids | An orientation lecture on company policies; a session on new tax rules |
| Conferences and seminars | Structured discussion and exchange of ideas among participants | A two-day seminar on industrial safety |
| Case study | Analysis of a real or hypothetical business situation to develop analytical and decision-making skills | Managers analyse a case of a failed product launch |
| Role playing | Participants act out roles in a simulated situation to practise interpersonal skills | A trainee practises handling an angry customer |
| Business games and simulations | Participants run a simulated company or operation and see the results of decisions | Teams compete in a marketing simulation |
| Vestibule training | Training on duplicate equipment in a separate area that reproduces the work environment | Cabin crew practise on a mock aircraft cabin; bank staff on a training branch |
| Programmed instruction and e-learning | Self-paced learning through structured modules with questions and feedback | An online module on information security completed by all staff |
| Sensitivity (T-group) training | Unstructured group interaction to increase self-awareness and interpersonal sensitivity | A laboratory programme for a leadership team |
| Outbound / experiential training | Outdoor activities designed to build teamwork and leadership | A team-building camp with problem-solving tasks |
| University and institute programmes | Formal courses and management development programmes | Executives attend a management programme at an IIM |

**Advantages:** learners can concentrate without work pressure; experts and specialised methods can be used; theory and concepts are taught systematically; large groups can be trained uniformly; mistakes are made safely; participants gain wider perspective through interaction with others.

**Limitations:** costly (trainers, venue, time away from work); the learning situation is artificial, so transfer to the job may be low unless supported; may not suit the specific context of each participant; production is lost while employees are away.

### 4. Comparison

| Basis | On-the-job | Off-the-job |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Actual workplace | Away from the workplace |
| Focus | Practical skills, "learning by doing" | Concepts, principles, attitudes, complex skills practised safely |
| Trainer | Supervisor or experienced worker | Professional trainers, experts |
| Cost | Low | High |
| Transfer of learning | Immediate and direct | Requires deliberate follow-up |
| Suitable for | Operatives, new recruits, skill upgrading | Managers, behavioural training, large groups, theory |

### 5. Conclusion

On-the-job methods such as coaching, job rotation, apprenticeship and job instruction training build practical skills in the real setting economically, while off-the-job methods such as lectures, case studies, role plays, simulations, vestibule training and e-learning develop knowledge, judgement and attitudes in a safe and focused environment. Most effective programmes blend the two - concepts learnt off the job are practised and reinforced on the job.

## Q44 | 8 | Explain e-learning as a training method and state its major advantages.

### 1. Meaning of e-learning

**E-learning** (electronic learning) is the delivery of training and education through digital technology - computers, the internet, intranets, learning management systems (LMS), mobile devices, virtual classrooms and multimedia - rather than solely through face-to-face instruction. It ranges from self-paced online modules and video lessons to live virtual classes, simulations, mobile micro-learning and blended programmes that combine online and classroom components. In the 21st century, employee training increasingly uses distance-learning alternatives such as web-based conferencing, web-based training and learning solutions deployed to mobile devices.

### 2. Forms of e-learning

| Form | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Self-paced online courses | Modules with text, video, quizzes and feedback that learners complete at their own speed | Compliance course on information security |
| Virtual instructor-led training (VILT) | Live classes conducted through video-conferencing tools with chat, polls and breakout rooms | A trainer in Mumbai teaches sales staff in twenty cities simultaneously |
| Learning management systems | Platforms that host courses, track completion, scores and certifications | Company LMS assigning role-based curricula |
| Simulations and virtual labs | Software that reproduces machines, processes or business situations for safe practice | Flight simulators; virtual chemistry labs; ERP sandbox training |
| Mobile and micro-learning | Short lessons (3-10 minutes) delivered on smartphones | Daily product-knowledge cards for field sales staff |
| Massive open online courses (MOOCs) and external platforms | University and vendor courses accessed online | Employees sponsored on Coursera, NPTEL or vendor certifications |
| Blended learning | Combination of e-learning with classroom and on-the-job components | Online theory followed by a practical workshop and coached practice |
| Electronic performance support systems (EPSS) | Just-in-time help, job aids and information embedded in work systems | Context-sensitive help inside a banking application |

### 3. How e-learning is used as a training method

The design process is the same as for any training - needs assessment, objectives, content, evaluation - but delivery is through technology. Good e-learning applies adult-learning principles: short, interactive modules; practice and immediate feedback; realistic scenarios; assessments that certify mastery; discussion forums and mentor support; and tracking through the LMS. It is most effective for knowledge and procedural content and, with simulations, for certain skills; it is usually blended with practice on the job for complex skills and with facilitated sessions for attitudes and interpersonal behaviour.

### 4. Major advantages of e-learning

1. **Accessibility - anytime, anywhere:** employees at remote locations, on different shifts or travelling can learn without coming to a training centre; interactive forums and tutorials reach even the most remote area.
2. **Cost-effectiveness:** once developed, a course can be delivered to thousands of employees without recurring costs of trainers, venues, travel and accommodation; time away from work is reduced.
3. **Consistency and standardisation:** every learner receives the same quality of content and message, which is important for compliance, safety and product knowledge.
4. **Self-paced and learner-centred:** learners proceed at their own speed, repeat difficult sections, skip what they already know and choose when to learn; this suits adult learners and different learning styles.
5. **Scalability and speed of deployment:** new content - a policy change, a product launch - can be rolled out to the whole organisation within days.
6. **Tracking and measurement:** the LMS records enrolment, completion, scores and time spent, providing data for evaluation, compliance reporting and learning analytics.
7. **Rich, engaging content:** multimedia, simulations, gamification and scenarios increase engagement and allow safe practice of risky or expensive tasks.
8. **Personalisation and just-in-time support:** adaptive paths, recommended courses and performance-support tools deliver the right content at the moment of need.
9. **Knowledge sharing and collaboration:** discussion boards, wikis, virtual communities and real-time conferencing allow sharing of knowledge, exchange of resources and learning across the organisation.
10. **Environmental and continuity benefits:** less travel and paper; learning continues during disruptions such as the pandemic.

### 5. Limitations to keep in mind

E-learning requires reliable technology and digital literacy; self-paced learning demands motivation and discipline, and drop-out rates can be high; it is less effective for hands-on skills and interpersonal behaviour unless blended; social interaction and immediate human feedback are reduced; and initial development costs for high-quality content can be significant.

### 6. Conclusion

E-learning is a technology-enabled training method that delivers learning through online modules, virtual classrooms, simulations, mobile content and learning platforms. Its major advantages - accessibility, cost-effectiveness, consistency, self-pacing, scalability, measurability and rich content - make it an essential part of modern HRD, especially when blended with on-the-job practice and facilitated sessions to overcome its limitations.

## Q45 | 8 | Explain the Kirkpatrick model for evaluating training effectiveness.

### 1. Introduction

Training does not end when the programme is over; its effectiveness must be judged against its objectives. The most widely used framework for this is the **Kirkpatrick model**, proposed by Donald L. Kirkpatrick in 1959 and refined over later decades. It evaluates training at **four successive levels** - Reaction, Learning, Behaviour and Results - each level asking a deeper question about the value of the training. The prescribed notes describe the same idea: trainees' reactions (affective outcomes) plus learning that occurred, behaviour changes and impact on corporate objectives.

### 2. The four levels

```
   Level 4  RESULTS     - What happened to the organisation?     (impact on business objectives)
   Level 3  BEHAVIOUR   - Are participants applying the learning? (change in on-the-job performance)
   Level 2  LEARNING    - What did participants learn?            (knowledge, skills, attitudes gained)
   Level 1  REACTION    - How did participants feel about it?     (satisfaction, relevance, engagement)
```

#### Level 1 - Reaction

- **Question:** Were participants satisfied? Did they find the programme relevant, engaging and well delivered?
- **Measures:** end-of-programme feedback forms ("happy sheets"), ratings of content, trainer, methods, facilities; verbal feedback; in the updated model, also engagement and perceived relevance.
- **Timing:** immediately after the programme.
- **Use:** improve programme design and delivery; a positive reaction is necessary for learning but does not prove that learning occurred.

#### Level 2 - Learning

- **Question:** To what extent did participants acquire the intended knowledge, skills and attitudes (and, in the updated model, confidence and commitment to apply them)?
- **Measures:** pre- and post-tests, skill demonstrations, simulations, role-play assessments, case analyses, certifications.
- **Timing:** at the end of the programme, ideally compared with a pre-training baseline or a control group.
- **Use:** verifies that the learning objectives were achieved.

#### Level 3 - Behaviour

- **Question:** Are participants applying what they learnt on the job? Has their behaviour or performance changed?
- **Measures:** observation by supervisors, performance appraisal data, 360-degree feedback, work samples, checklists, interviews with participants and their managers, self-reports of application.
- **Timing:** three to six months after training, to allow opportunity for application.
- **Use:** reveals whether transfer of learning has occurred; failure here usually indicates lack of opportunity, supervisor support, rewards or a supportive climate rather than poor training.

#### Level 4 - Results

- **Question:** What measurable organisational outcomes resulted from the training?
- **Measures:** productivity, quality and defect rates, sales, costs, customer satisfaction, accident rates, turnover, absenteeism, time to competence; compared against pre-training baselines and, where possible, control groups; sometimes extended to return on investment (Phillips' Level 5).
- **Timing:** six to twelve months after training.
- **Use:** demonstrates the business value of training to management and justifies the investment.

### 3. Summary table

| Level | Focus | Typical tools | When | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Reaction | Satisfaction and relevance | Feedback forms, discussions | Immediately | Improve delivery |
| 2 Learning | KSAs acquired | Tests, demonstrations, simulations | End of programme | Confirm objectives met |
| 3 Behaviour | Application on the job | Observation, appraisal, 360-degree feedback | 3-6 months later | Check transfer of learning |
| 4 Results | Organisational impact | Business metrics, ROI | 6-12 months later | Prove value, guide investment |

### 4. Key principles of the model

- The levels form a **chain of evidence**: each higher level gives more valuable but harder-to-collect information.
- Evaluation should be **planned during design**, starting with the results desired (the updated model recommends working backwards from Level 4).
- Positive results at a lower level do not guarantee results at a higher level; a programme may be enjoyed (Level 1) and learnt (Level 2) but never applied (Level 3).
- Levels 3 and 4 depend not only on the training but on the **work environment** - supervisor support, opportunity to apply, rewards and organisational climate.

### 5. Example

A customer-service programme is evaluated as follows: participants rate it 4.6/5 (Reaction); average test scores rise from 55 to 85 per cent (Learning); mystery-shopper audits three months later show 40 per cent more compliant service behaviours (Behaviour); customer-satisfaction scores rise from 72 to 81 and complaints fall by 30 per cent over the next year (Results).

### 6. Conclusion

The Kirkpatrick model evaluates training at four levels - Reaction, Learning, Behaviour and Results - moving from how participants felt to what they learnt, what they now do differently and what the organisation gained. It provides a simple, systematic and widely accepted way of judging whether a training programme was merely enjoyable or genuinely effective.

## Q46 | 8 | Describe the importance of delivering a training program effectively after it has been designed.

### 1. Meaning of training delivery

**Training delivery (implementation)** is the stage of the training cycle in which the designed programme is actually put into effect according to the plan. It has two segments: **practical administrative arrangements** (venue, materials, equipment, schedules, participants, trainers) and **carrying out the training** (facilitating the sessions so that learning happens). A design exists on paper; delivery is where trainees meet the content, the trainer and one another. However good the design, it is the quality of delivery that decides whether learning objectives are achieved.

### 2. Why effective delivery is important

1. **Design produces learning only through delivery.** Objectives, content and methods are potential value; delivery converts them into actual knowledge, skills and attitudes. A weak delivery wastes all the effort and cost of TNA and design.
2. **Learner engagement and motivation.** Adults learn when they are involved, respected and see relevance. A skilled trainer creates interest, links content to participants' real problems, uses participative methods and maintains energy; a poor trainer loses the group in the first hour.
3. **Creating a learning environment.** Effective delivery provides psychological safety to ask questions and make mistakes, appropriate pace, practice opportunities and immediate feedback - conditions identified in the notes as essential for learning.
4. **Adaptation to the group.** No design fits every group perfectly. During delivery the trainer reads the participants' level and adjusts examples, depth, speed and activities in real time.
5. **Transfer of learning begins in the classroom.** Delivery that uses realistic practice, action planning and discussion of workplace barriers prepares participants to apply the learning on the job; delivery that stays theoretical does not.
6. **Credibility of the HRD function.** Participants judge the entire HRD system by their experience in the room. Well-run programmes build trust in HRD and willingness to attend future programmes; badly run ones create cynicism ("training is a waste of time").
7. **Efficient use of resources.** Smooth administration - materials ready, equipment working, sessions on time, participants informed in advance - avoids loss of costly trainer and participant time.
8. **Valid evaluation.** Evaluation at reaction, learning, behaviour and results levels assumes that the programme was delivered as designed. Poor delivery makes it impossible to know whether the design or the execution failed.
9. **Consistency across batches and locations.** Effective delivery systems (trainer guides, train-the-trainer, quality checks) ensure that every batch receives the same quality, which is essential for compliance, safety and customer-facing skills.
10. **Return on investment.** Training is an investment; effective delivery is what makes the return possible.

### 3. Elements of effective delivery

| Element | Good practice |
|---|---|
| Administrative readiness | Venue and equipment checked; materials printed or uploaded; participants and their managers informed of objectives; schedule fits work shifts |
| Trainer competence | Subject expertise plus facilitation skills - communication, questioning, handling group dynamics, giving feedback |
| Opening | Set objectives and expectations, establish relevance, create a safe climate, agree ground rules |
| Methods in action | Mix of explanation, demonstration, practice, discussion, cases, role plays; short segments; frequent interaction |
| Learning principles | Build on experience, move from known to unknown, provide practice and reinforcement, give immediate feedback, respect individual pace |
| Managing time and energy | Breaks, variety, energisers, keeping to the plan while allowing useful discussion |
| Closing | Summary, action plans for application, evaluation forms, information about follow-up support |
| Follow-up | Reminders, job aids, coaching by supervisors, review of action plans after a few weeks |

### 4. Common delivery failures

Lecturing for hours, reading slides, ignoring participants' experience, no practice, technology failures, wrong participants, overloaded content, no link to the job, and no follow-up. Each of these can undo a good design.

### 5. Conclusion

Delivery is the moment of truth for a training programme. Effective delivery - careful administration, a competent facilitator, an engaging and safe learning environment, appropriate methods and preparation for transfer - turns a sound design into real learning, protects the investment made in TNA and design, builds the credibility of HRD and makes meaningful evaluation possible. Design decides what could be learnt; delivery decides what is learnt.

## Q47 | 8 | Explain the relationship among Training Needs Assessment, training design, training delivery, and training evaluation.

### 1. Introduction

Training is best understood as a **cycle of four stages** - Training Needs Assessment (TNA), design, delivery (implementation) and evaluation - rather than as a set of separate activities. Each stage depends on the one before it and provides the basis for the one after it, and the last stage feeds back into the first. This systems view is common to the four-stage training cycle in the prescribed notes and the HRD process model of Werner and DeSimone (assessment - design - implementation - evaluation).

### 2. The training cycle

```
        +-------------------------------------------------------------------+
        |                                                                   |
        v                                                                   |
   1. TRAINING NEEDS      2. TRAINING          3. TRAINING          4. TRAINING
      ASSESSMENT     -->     DESIGN       -->     DELIVERY      -->     EVALUATION
   (what gap? who?        (objectives,         (administration,      (reaction, learning,
    why? priority?)        content, methods,    facilitation,          behaviour, results)
                           trainers, budget,    learning climate)              |
                           evaluation plan)                                    |
        ^                                                                      |
        +---------------------- feedback and improvement ----------------------+
```

### 3. How each stage depends on the others

#### TNA -> Design

TNA identifies the performance gap, its causes, the people affected and the priority. Design converts these into a training goal, learning objectives, content, methods and participant selection. Without TNA, design has no valid basis - objectives become guesses and content becomes generic. TNA also provides the **baseline** (actual performance) against which evaluation will later measure improvement.

#### Design -> Delivery

Design produces the plan - lesson plans, materials, methods, trainers, schedule, budget - that delivery executes. A clear design allows consistent delivery across batches and gives the trainer a road map, while still leaving room to adapt to the group. Poor design (unclear objectives, overloaded content, wrong methods) makes even skilled delivery ineffective.

#### Delivery -> Evaluation

Evaluation measures what delivery achieved: participants' reactions, learning gained, behaviour changed and results produced. Delivery must therefore be faithful to the design so that evaluation results are interpretable, and the trainer collects Level 1 and Level 2 data during delivery itself.

#### Design -> Evaluation (planned together)

Evaluation criteria and methods are decided during design, derived from the learning objectives. Objectives written in measurable terms ("will be able to ... to a standard of ...") are what make Level 2 and Level 3 evaluation possible. The Kirkpatrick approach recommends designing backwards from the desired results.

#### Evaluation -> TNA and design (the feedback loop)

Evaluation findings show whether the need was correctly identified, whether the design was appropriate, whether delivery was effective and whether the organisation supported transfer. Gaps found at Level 3 or 4 become inputs to the next TNA; weaknesses at Level 1 or 2 lead to redesign or changes in delivery. Evaluation thus closes the loop and starts the next cycle.

### 4. Relationship shown as inputs and outputs

| Stage | Main inputs (from) | Main outputs (to) |
|---|---|---|
| TNA | Organisational goals, appraisal data, performance records, previous evaluations | Training needs, priorities, target group, baseline data -> design and evaluation |
| Design | Needs and objectives from TNA | Training plan: objectives, content, methods, materials, trainers, schedule, budget, evaluation plan -> delivery and evaluation |
| Delivery | Training plan and materials | Learning experience, Level 1 and 2 data, action plans -> evaluation and the workplace |
| Evaluation | Objectives and baseline from TNA and design; data from delivery and the job | Judgement of effectiveness, lessons for improvement -> next TNA and design |

### 5. Consequences of breaking the chain

Skipping TNA produces irrelevant training; designing without measurable objectives makes evaluation impossible; delivering without following the design makes results uninterpretable; and not evaluating means the same mistakes are repeated and the value of training cannot be shown. Rao and Abraham's finding that many organisations sponsor employees without genuine need analysis and give little opportunity to apply learning is an example of a broken cycle.

### 6. Conclusion

TNA, design, delivery and evaluation are four interdependent stages of a single cycle. TNA defines the need and baseline; design converts the need into a plan and evaluation criteria; delivery executes the plan and generates learning; evaluation measures the outcome against the objectives and feeds lessons back into the next cycle. Training is effective only when all four are connected.

## Q48 | 8 | Discuss the importance of evaluating training effectiveness for employees and organizations.

### 1. Meaning of training evaluation

**Training evaluation** is the systematic collection and analysis of information to judge whether a training programme achieved its objectives and produced value. The training process does not end when the programme is completed; its effectiveness must be evaluated against outcomes related to the programme's objectives - typically the four Kirkpatrick levels of Reaction, Learning, Behaviour and Results, and sometimes return on investment. Evaluation matters to two parties: the employees who were trained and the organisation that paid for the training.

### 2. Importance for employees

1. **Confirmation of learning and competence** - tests, demonstrations and certifications tell employees that they have actually acquired the skill, which builds confidence and self-efficacy.
2. **Feedback for further improvement** - evaluation identifies what each participant still needs to master, guiding coaching and further development.
3. **Recognition and career progress** - documented learning and improved performance become evidence for appraisal, potential assessment, promotion and career planning.
4. **Relevance of future training** - participants' reactions and behaviour data improve subsequent programmes, so employees receive better, more useful training.
5. **Support for application** - Level 3 evaluation reveals barriers to using new skills (lack of tools, unsupportive supervisors), which the organisation can then remove; this helps employees succeed with their new learning.
6. **Motivation** - knowing that learning will be measured and its application noticed encourages employees to take training seriously and to apply it.
7. **Voice** - reaction evaluation gives employees a say in the design and quality of their development.

### 3. Importance for the organisation

1. **Accountability and justification of investment** - training costs money and time; evaluation shows management what it received in return and supports future budgets.
2. **Decision making** - evaluation results guide decisions on whether to continue, repeat, modify or discontinue a programme, whether to retain a trainer or vendor, whether to reuse methods and how to budget.
3. **Improvement of design and delivery** - Level 1 and 2 data pinpoint weaknesses in content, methods, trainers or logistics.
4. **Verification of transfer** - Level 3 data show whether learning is being applied; if not, the organisation can address supervisory support, opportunity and rewards - the real determinants of transfer.
5. **Link to business results** - Level 4 data connect training to productivity, quality, sales, safety, customer satisfaction and turnover, demonstrating HRD's strategic contribution and building the credibility and respectability of the function.
6. **Validation of the needs assessment** - if results do not improve despite good learning, the need may have been wrongly diagnosed or non-training causes may exist; evaluation corrects the next TNA.
7. **Organisational learning and knowledge** - evaluation builds a database of what works for whom, improving the whole HRD system over time.
8. **Risk management and compliance** - documented evidence that employees were trained and assessed is essential for safety, quality and regulatory requirements.
9. **Motivation of trainers and line managers** - measurement creates accountability for results rather than for activity.

### 4. Different stakeholders value different measures

Senior and line managers tend to judge HRD by quantitative measures such as better utilisation of people, productivity and flexibility; HRD specialists look at programme quality and participation; individual learners care about employability, certification and competencies gained. A good evaluation therefore reports at multiple levels to satisfy all three groups.

### 5. Consequences of not evaluating

Training becomes an act of faith; ineffective programmes continue and effective ones are cut in downturns; transfer problems remain hidden; employees receive no feedback; and HRD cannot demonstrate its value or improve. D'Souza's observation that the return on training investment in many Indian industries is poor is partly a result of the absence of evaluation.

### 6. Conclusion

Evaluating training effectiveness is important for employees because it confirms their learning, guides their further development, supports their careers and removes barriers to applying new skills; and for the organisation because it justifies investment, improves programmes, verifies transfer, links training to business results and strengthens the entire HRD system. Without evaluation, training remains an expense of unknown value; with it, training becomes a managed investment.

### 1. Applying the TNA concept to the case

Repeated performance errors are a **symptom**, not a diagnosis. The purpose of Training Needs Assessment here is to establish (a) exactly what the errors are and what standard is being missed, (b) why they occur, (c) whether the causes are training-related (knowledge, skill, attitude) or non-training-related (systems, tools, workload, motivation, unclear standards), and (d) if training is required, who needs what. The information to be collected is organised below by the three levels of TNA plus a cause analysis.

### 2. Information to collect at the organisation level

| Information | Why it is needed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nature, frequency, cost and trend of the errors | Defines the size and priority of the problem and the baseline for later evaluation | Error logs, quality reports, customer complaints, rework and scrap data |

| Recent changes | New systems, products, procedures, staff turnover or restructuring often trigger errors | Management interviews, change records |
| Departmental climate and management support | Fear, overload or poor supervision may cause errors and will affect transfer of any training | HRD climate survey, employee interviews |

| Information | Why it is needed | Source |
|---|---|---|

| Knowledge, skills and attitudes (KSAs) required for each critical task | Defines the competencies training would have to build | Task analysis, subject-matter experts |

| Tools, equipment, information and job aids available | Reveals whether tasks are workable without error | Observation, equipment records |

|---|---|---|

```
 Can the employee do the task correctly if his or her life depended on it?
```

Before designing training for a department with repeated errors, TNA requires information at three levels - the organisational context, standards and error data; the tasks and competencies involved; and the individuals, their current capability, their explanations and their working conditions - together with a cause analysis that separates training from non-training problems. Collecting this information ensures that the training, if needed, targets the real gap, reaches the right people and can be evaluated against a known baseline.

## Q50 | 8 | Apply suitable training methods to design a development program for employees who need both practical job skills and conceptual knowledge.

### 1. Understanding the requirement

The employees need two different kinds of learning. **Conceptual knowledge** - principles, theories, procedures, "why" - is best learnt through methods that explain, illustrate and allow discussion, typically off the job or through e-learning. **Practical job skills** - the "how" - are learnt through demonstration, practice, feedback and repetition, typically on the job or in simulated settings. Neither method type alone will do: pure classroom training produces people who know but cannot do, while pure on-the-job training produces people who can do but do not understand, and therefore cannot adapt when situations change. The design must therefore be a **blended programme** in which concepts and skills reinforce each other.

### 2. Design principles applied

- Sequence **concept -> demonstration -> practice -> application -> review** for each module.
- Use the **70-20-10 idea** as a guide: most learning through on-the-job experience, a substantial part through coaching and social learning, and a focused part through formal instruction.
- Keep formal sessions short and spaced so that practice happens between them.
- Involve supervisors as coaches to ensure transfer.
- Evaluate at all four Kirkpatrick levels.

### 3. Methods selected and their purpose

| Learning need | Method | Why it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Foundational concepts and principles | E-learning modules and short classroom lectures with visuals | Efficient, consistent, self-paced delivery of knowledge; can be revisited |
| Understanding application of concepts | Case studies and group discussion | Develop analysis and judgement; connect theory to realistic situations |
| Procedures and standards | Demonstration by expert and job aids / checklists | Shows the correct way; job aids support recall on the job |
| Basic skill practice in a safe setting | Vestibule / simulation training, role plays | Practice without risk to production, customers or safety; immediate feedback |
| Skill building on real work | Job instruction training and coaching by supervisor | Real conditions, real feedback, immediate transfer |
| Breadth and system understanding | Job rotation and cross-functional project | Shows how tasks connect; builds conceptual understanding through experience |
| Reflection and deeper learning | Action learning sets, learning journals, peer review | Convert experience into insight; reinforce concepts |
| Guidance and motivation | Mentoring | Sustains development and links learning to career |

### 4. Structure of the development programme (illustrative 12-week design)

```
 WEEK 1     Orientation: objectives, assessment of current knowledge and skills (baseline)
            E-learning Module 1 (core concepts) + half-day classroom session with case discussion

 WEEKS 2-3  Demonstration of key procedures by expert; vestibule / simulation practice
            Job aids issued; supervisor briefed on coaching plan

 WEEKS 4-6  On-the-job practice under job instruction training; daily coaching feedback
            E-learning Module 2 (advanced concepts) completed during the period
            Weekly one-hour review session: problems faced, concepts revisited

 WEEKS 7-9  Job rotation to a related section (2 weeks) to see the whole process
            Case study workshop applying concepts to real departmental problems
            Skill assessment 1 (checklist-based observation)

 WEEKS 10-11 Small improvement project in own area applying both concepts and skills
            Mentor meeting; action learning set presentation

 WEEK 12    Final knowledge test and skill certification; action plan for continued application
            Level 1 and Level 2 evaluation; schedule Level 3 review at 3 months and Level 4 at 6-12 months
```

### 5. Roles

- **HRD department:** designs modules, e-learning and cases; trains supervisors as coaches; runs assessments.
- **Subject experts / internal trainers:** demonstrate procedures and lead workshops.
- **Supervisors:** coach daily, sign off skill checklists, provide opportunities to apply.
- **Mentors:** guide development and career linkage.
- **Participants:** complete e-learning, practise, keep learning journals, deliver the project.

### 6. Evaluation plan

Reaction forms after each formal session; pre/post knowledge tests and skill certification (Learning); supervisor observation checklists and error/quality data three months later (Behaviour); departmental productivity, quality, customer or safety indicators after six to twelve months (Results).

### 7. Conclusion

A development programme for employees who need both conceptual knowledge and practical skills must blend methods: e-learning, lectures and case studies for concepts; demonstration, simulation, job instruction training and coaching for skills; and job rotation, projects and mentoring to integrate the two. Sequenced as concept, demonstration, practice, application and review, supported by supervisors and evaluated at all levels, such a blended design produces employees who both understand their work and can perform it well.

## Q51 | 8 | State any two purposes of Training Needs Assessment.

### 1. Meaning of Training Needs Assessment

Training Needs Assessment (TNA) is the systematic process of identifying the gap between the performance and competence an organisation requires and what its employees currently possess (Training need = Standard performance - Actual performance), analysing the causes of the gap, and deciding whether and how training should address it. It is conducted at three levels - organisation, task and person - and is the first stage of the training cycle. TNA serves several purposes; two of the most important are explained in detail below, followed by the others.

### 2. Purpose 1 - To identify the real performance gap and determine whether training is the right solution

- Organisations often assume that every performance problem needs training. TNA tests this assumption by measuring the gap between expected and actual performance and analysing its **causes**: lack of knowledge or skill (training problems) versus poor tools, unclear standards, weak feedback, overload, wrong incentives or low motivation (non-training problems).
- By separating the two, TNA ensures that training is prescribed only where it will work, and that other problems are referred to the right remedy - system redesign, better supervision, clearer goals or counselling.
- This purpose protects the organisation from the common error of buying "off-the-shelf" programmes without considering their relevance or applicability, and from the frustration of employees who are trained for problems that training cannot solve.
- **Example:** A TNA of billing errors finds that half the errors arise from a confusing software screen (system problem) and half from new staff not knowing the returns policy (training need). Training is designed only for the second, while IT redesigns the screen for the first.

### 3. Purpose 2 - To define who needs what training and to provide the basis for objectives, design and evaluation

- Once a genuine training need is confirmed, TNA specifies the **target group** (person analysis), the **content** in terms of knowledge, skills and attitudes required (task analysis) and the **priority and business linkage** (organisation analysis).
- These findings become the **learning objectives** of the programme and guide decisions on methods, trainers, duration and budget - the design stage cannot begin properly without them.
- TNA also records the **baseline** of actual performance, against which the programme is later evaluated at the Kirkpatrick levels of learning, behaviour and results. Without a baseline, effectiveness cannot be demonstrated.
- Selecting participants on the basis of assessed need - rather than as a reward, a routine or a "fringe benefit" - was identified by Rao and Abraham as one of the weakest links in Indian training practice; this purpose of TNA corrects it.
- **Example:** A TNA shows that only supervisors on the night shift lack knowledge of the new safety procedure; a targeted two-hour programme for those twelve people, with a pre/post test and an accident-rate baseline, replaces a company-wide seminar.

### 4. Other purposes of TNA

| Purpose | Brief explanation |
|---|---|
| Link training to organisational strategy | Organisation analysis ties programmes to business goals and future capability requirements |
| Prioritise and allocate resources | Needs are ranked by impact, urgency and numbers so that limited budgets go where they matter most |
| Build readiness and commitment | Employees and managers involved in the assessment are more motivated to learn and to support application |
| Assess readiness of the environment | Reveals whether the climate, supervisors and resources will support transfer of training |
| Integrate training with other HRD subsystems | Draws on appraisal, counselling and potential appraisal; feeds career and succession planning |
| Prepare for change | Identifies the capabilities required before new technology, processes or structures are introduced |

### 5. Conclusion

Two central purposes of Training Needs Assessment are, first, to identify the real performance gap and establish whether training - rather than some other intervention - is the right solution, and second, to define precisely who needs what training so that objectives, design and evaluation rest on a sound foundation. Together with its other purposes, TNA ensures that training is relevant, targeted, economical and measurable.

## Q52 | 8 | Name any two levels of the Kirkpatrick model.

### 1. The Kirkpatrick model in brief

The Kirkpatrick model, developed by Donald L. Kirkpatrick in 1959, is the most widely used framework for evaluating training effectiveness. It assesses a programme at **four successive levels** - **Level 1 Reaction, Level 2 Learning, Level 3 Behaviour and Level 4 Results** - each answering a deeper question about the value of the training. Any two levels may be named; two are explained in detail below, and the other two are summarised so that the complete model is clear.

### 2. Level 1 - Reaction

- **What it measures:** how participants felt about the training - their satisfaction with the content, trainer, methods, materials, facilities and organisation, and (in the updated model) their engagement and the perceived relevance of the programme to their jobs.
- **How it is measured:** end-of-programme feedback questionnaires (often called "happy sheets") with rating scales and open comments; verbal feedback; observation of participation.
- **When:** immediately at the end of the programme or of each session.
- **Why it matters:** a favourable reaction creates the motivation to learn and indicates whether the delivery met participants' expectations; feedback is used to improve future programmes. However, a positive reaction does not prove that learning or performance improvement occurred - it is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Example:** Participants rate a negotiation-skills workshop 4.5 out of 5 for usefulness and 4.8 for trainer effectiveness, but suggest more practice time.

### 3. Level 2 - Learning

- **What it measures:** the extent to which participants acquired the intended knowledge, skills and attitudes - and, in the updated model, the confidence and commitment to apply them.
- **How it is measured:** pre-tests and post-tests, skill demonstrations, simulations, role-play assessments with checklists, case analyses, projects and certifications; where possible, comparison with a control group.
- **When:** at the end of the programme, compared with a pre-training baseline.
- **Why it matters:** it verifies that the learning objectives set during design were achieved. If learning has not occurred, behaviour change and results cannot follow; if it has occurred but behaviour does not change, the problem lies in the work environment rather than the training.
- **Example:** Average scores on a product-knowledge test rise from 52 per cent before training to 88 per cent after; 90 per cent of participants demonstrate the correct procedure in a simulated exercise.

### 4. The remaining two levels

| Level | Question | Typical measures | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 3 - Behaviour | Are participants applying the learning on the job? | Supervisor observation, appraisal data, 360-degree feedback, work samples | 3-6 months after training |
| Level 4 - Results | What organisational outcomes resulted? | Productivity, quality, sales, costs, customer satisfaction, safety, turnover; return on investment | 6-12 months after training |

### 5. The logic of the levels

```
   Reaction  -->  Learning  -->  Behaviour  -->  Results
   (felt)         (know/can)     (do)            (organisation gains)
   easy and quick to measure  ---------->  harder, slower, but more valuable evidence
```

Each higher level provides stronger evidence of effectiveness but is more difficult and costly to measure. Good evaluation plans decide the levels during the design stage and, ideally, work backwards from the results desired.

### 6. Conclusion

Two levels of the Kirkpatrick model are Level 1 Reaction, which measures participants' satisfaction and perceived relevance, and Level 2 Learning, which measures the knowledge, skills and attitudes actually acquired. Together with Level 3 Behaviour and Level 4 Results, they form a chain of evidence that tells an organisation whether a training programme was merely well received or truly effective.

## Q53 | 8 | Analyze why a training program may receive positive participant reactions but still fail to improve job performance.

### 1. The issue

In Kirkpatrick's terms this is a programme that scores high at **Level 1 (Reaction)** but low at **Level 3 (Behaviour)** and **Level 4 (Results)**. It is a very common pattern: participants enjoy the programme, praise the trainer and rate it as useful, yet three months later nothing has changed on the job. The reasons lie partly in the programme itself and, more often, in the surrounding HRD system and work environment. The analysis below groups the causes into four areas.

### 2. Causes related to needs assessment and design

1. **Wrong diagnosis - training was not the solution.** If the performance problem was caused by poor tools, unclear standards, overload or lack of incentives, even excellent training cannot fix it. Participants may enjoy the programme, but the real cause remains.
2. **Generic or irrelevant content.** Programmes bought "off the shelf" are often entertaining and well presented but not tied to the specific tasks, systems and standards of the participants' jobs.
3. **Wrong participants.** People sent as a reward or to fill seats react positively (a pleasant break from work) but had no need and therefore nothing to change.
4. **Objectives set at the knowledge level only.** If the design aimed at awareness rather than at demonstrable skill, participants leave informed but not able.
5. **Insufficient practice.** Skills require repeated practice with feedback; a programme heavy on lectures, videos and discussion may be enjoyable yet build no capability.

### 3. Causes related to learning itself

6. **Enjoyment is not learning.** Positive reactions reflect entertainment value, trainer charisma, good food and a comfortable venue as much as learning. Level 2 tests may reveal that little was actually retained.
7. **No measurement of learning.** Without pre/post assessment, nobody knows whether the objectives were achieved.

### 4. Causes related to transfer of learning and the work environment

8. **Lack of opportunity to apply.** Rao and Abraham found that many employees had little chance to try out what they learnt; skills not used within weeks decay rapidly.
9. **Unsupportive supervisors.** If the boss did not brief the participant before training, does not expect new behaviour afterwards and does not coach, the old ways return.
10. **Peer and cultural pressure.** Colleagues may ridicule or resist new methods ("that is not how we do it here"); a weak HRD climate discourages experimentation.
11. **No reinforcement through appraisal and rewards.** When appraisal criteria and incentives still reward old behaviour, the new behaviour has no pay-off.
12. **Systems and resources unchanged.** New skills may need new tools, authority, information or time that the workplace does not provide.
13. **No follow-up.** Absence of action plans, refresher sessions, job aids or coaching after the programme leaves participants alone with the difficulty of changing habits.
14. **Time lag and workload.** Back at work, urgent tasks crowd out the effort needed to apply learning consciously.

### 5. Causes related to the HRD system

15. **Training isolated from other subsystems.** Without linkage to appraisal, counselling, career planning and rewards, training is an event rather than part of a development process.
16. **Evaluation stops at Level 1.** When organisations measure only reactions, trainers optimise for satisfaction rather than for performance change, and transfer problems are never detected or addressed.

### 6. Diagrammatic summary

```
 High REACTION  --x-->  LEARNING?   --x-->  BEHAVIOUR?   --x-->  RESULTS?
                   |                   |                    |
        entertainment, not        no opportunity,       wrong diagnosis,
        skill; no practice;       no supervisor          unchanged systems,
        wrong participants        support, no rewards,   incentives, culture
                                  no follow-up
```

### 7. What should be done

Conduct proper TNA and cause analysis; set behaviour-level objectives with practice; select participants by need; brief supervisors before and involve them after; require action plans and follow-up coaching; align appraisal and rewards with the trained behaviour; provide tools and opportunity; build an experimenting, supportive climate; and evaluate at Levels 2, 3 and 4, not only Level 1.

### 8. Conclusion

A training programme can be liked and still fail because reaction measures enjoyment, not capability or application. The failure usually lies in a wrong or generic diagnosis, insufficient practice, the wrong participants and, above all, a work environment that offers no opportunity, support, reinforcement or reward for the new behaviour. Positive reactions are the beginning of effectiveness, not the proof of it.

## Q54 | 8 | Analyze the consequences of designing a training program without conducting an adequate Training Needs Assessment.

### 1. Introduction

Training Needs Assessment (TNA) is the diagnostic first stage of the training cycle. It establishes the gap between required and actual performance, identifies its causes, decides whether training is the right remedy, specifies who needs what, and records the baseline for evaluation. Organisations skip or shorten it because it seems time-consuming, costly, requires skilled staff, or because managers do not want to confront the real reasons for poor performance. The consequences of this short cut affect every later stage of the training process and the HRD system as a whole, as analysed below.

### 2. Consequences for the design of the programme

1. **Vague or wrong objectives.** Without a defined gap, objectives are written in general terms ("improve communication") that cannot guide content or be measured.
2. **Irrelevant or generic content.** Content is chosen from what trainers offer rather than from what tasks require; "off-the-shelf" programmes are bought without checking their applicability.
3. **Inappropriate methods and level.** Not knowing participants' existing knowledge leads to programmes that are too basic for some and too advanced for others; skills that need practice are taught by lecture.
4. **Wrong duration and cost.** Programmes are longer or shorter than the need requires; budgets are spent without relation to impact.

### 3. Consequences for participants

5. **Wrong participants.** People are nominated by convenience, seniority or as a reward rather than by need; those who need training are left out while others attend unnecessarily.
6. **Low motivation and cynicism.** Employees who see no connection between the programme and their work disengage, and future programmes are treated as holidays.
7. **Frustration.** Employees trained for problems caused by systems, tools or policies return to the same obstacles and conclude that training changes nothing.

### 4. Consequences for performance and the organisation

8. **The real problem remains unsolved.** If the errors were caused by faulty equipment, unclear standards, overload or poor incentives, training cannot remove them; the performance gap persists and management concludes that "training does not work."
9. **Wasted resources.** Trainer fees, materials, venues and, above all, participants' productive time are spent without return - the "fringe benefit" training with poor return on investment noted by D'Souza.
10. **Strategic capability gaps continue.** Without organisation-level analysis, training is not aligned to business goals; skills the strategy needs are not built while budgets are consumed elsewhere.
11. **Low transfer of learning.** Since the environment was never assessed, barriers to application - unsupportive supervisors, lack of opportunity - are not addressed.
12. **Risk and compliance exposure.** Safety, quality or regulatory training needs may be missed altogether.

### 5. Consequences for evaluation and the HRD system

13. **Evaluation becomes impossible or meaningless.** Without a baseline and measurable objectives, effectiveness cannot be judged at the learning, behaviour or results levels; evaluation stops at reaction forms.
14. **No organisational learning.** Because causes were never analysed, the same programmes are repeated year after year with the same lack of effect.
15. **Loss of credibility of HRD.** Management sees activity without results; training budgets are cut in the next downturn; the HRD function loses status and influence.
16. **Broken linkages with other subsystems.** Training that does not draw on appraisal, counselling and potential data cannot feed career and succession planning; the HRD system fragments.

### 6. Chain of consequences

```
 No / weak TNA
    -> wrong diagnosis, vague objectives, generic content, wrong participants
    -> little learning that matters, low motivation
    -> no change in job performance; real causes untouched
    -> no measurable results; wasted budget
    -> "training does not work"  -> reduced investment  -> weaker HRD system
```

### 7. Illustration

A company facing customer complaints about delayed deliveries sent all warehouse staff to a customer-service programme. Participants enjoyed it. Complaints continued because the delays were caused by an outdated stock-location system and unrealistic despatch targets - facts that a TNA would have revealed in a week. The training budget was spent, the problem remained, and staff lost faith in HRD.

### 8. Conclusion

Designing training without an adequate TNA produces programmes with unclear objectives, irrelevant content and the wrong participants; it leaves the real causes of poor performance untouched, wastes resources, makes evaluation meaningless and undermines the credibility of the entire HRD function. The time and cost of a proper TNA are small compared with the cost of training that solves nothing.

## Q55 | 8 | Evaluate the usefulness of the Kirkpatrick model for judging the effectiveness of an organizational training program.

### 1. The model in brief

The Kirkpatrick model (1959, updated as the "New World Kirkpatrick Model") evaluates training at four levels: **Reaction** (satisfaction and relevance), **Learning** (knowledge, skills, attitudes, confidence and commitment acquired), **Behaviour** (application on the job) and **Results** (organisational outcomes). It is by far the most widely used evaluation framework in HRD practice. The question is how useful it actually is for judging the effectiveness of an organisational training programme. The evaluation below weighs its strengths against its limitations and reaches a balanced judgement.

### 2. Strengths - why the model is useful

1. **Simplicity and clarity.** Four levels with plain-language questions make the model easy to understand and communicate to trainers, line managers and top management alike.
2. **Comprehensive chain of evidence.** It moves systematically from participants' feelings to organisational impact, preventing the common error of judging training by reactions alone.
3. **Links training to business results.** Level 4 forces HRD to define and measure the organisational outcomes a programme is meant to influence, which supports strategy alignment and demonstrates HRD's value.
4. **Diagnostic power.** Comparing levels shows where a programme breaks down: high learning but low behaviour points to transfer problems in the work environment rather than to the training itself.
5. **Guides design.** Planning evaluation from Level 4 backwards produces clearer objectives, better methods and built-in transfer support.
6. **Flexibility.** The model can be applied to technical, behavioural, management and e-learning programmes, at any scale, with methods ranging from simple forms to controlled studies.
7. **Common language and benchmarking.** Its universal use allows comparison across programmes, vendors and organisations and supports HRD audits.
8. **Involves line managers.** Levels 3 and 4 require supervisors to observe behaviour and supply performance data, drawing them into responsibility for transfer.

### 3. Limitations - where the model falls short

| Limitation | Explanation | How serious? |
|---|---|---|
| Assumes a causal chain | It implies that reaction leads to learning, learning to behaviour and behaviour to results; research shows the correlations between levels are weak | Moderate - the levels should be treated as different kinds of evidence, not as a guaranteed sequence |
| Ignores context and process | It evaluates outcomes but says little about why they occurred - needs assessment quality, design, delivery, climate, supervisor support | Significant - must be supplemented with process evaluation |
| Higher levels are difficult and costly | Isolating training's effect on business results from other factors (market, technology, other initiatives) requires baselines, control groups and time; most organisations stop at Level 1 or 2 | Practical constraint rather than a flaw of the model |
| No cost dimension | It does not compare benefits with costs; Phillips added a fifth level, Return on Investment, for this | Easily remedied by adding ROI |
| Focus on individual training events | Less suited to continuous, informal, social and on-the-job learning or to broader HRD interventions such as OD and culture change | Moderate |
| Time lag and attribution | Results appear months later and may be attributed to other causes; managers may lose interest | Requires planning and communication |
| Possible over-simplification | Reduces complex learning and performance to four boxes; may neglect unintended outcomes, learner well-being or long-term career effects | Minor if used thoughtfully |

### 4. Comparison with alternatives

Other frameworks - the CIRO model (Context, Input, Reaction, Outcome), Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method, Kaufman's five levels and Phillips' ROI methodology - address some gaps, especially context and cost. Yet most of them build on Kirkpatrick's levels rather than replace them, which is itself evidence of the model's usefulness.

### 5. Conditions for getting the most from the model

- Decide the desired results and leading indicators **before** design; write behaviour-level objectives.
- Measure Levels 1 and 2 for every programme, Level 3 for important programmes, Level 4 for strategic or expensive programmes.
- Use baselines from the TNA, comparison groups where feasible and multiple data sources.
- Evaluate process factors (design quality, delivery, supervisor support, climate) alongside outcomes.
- Add ROI or cost-benefit analysis for major investments.
- Report results to line managers and top management and act on them.

### 6. Judgement

For an organisational training programme, the Kirkpatrick model is **highly useful as an organising framework**: it defines what to measure, in what order and why, keeps attention on behaviour and business results, and provides a common language. It is **less useful as a complete explanation** of why a programme succeeded or failed and it does not by itself handle cost or context. Its practical weakness - that organisations rarely go beyond Level 2 - is a failure of application rather than of the model.

### 7. Conclusion

The Kirkpatrick model remains the most practical and widely accepted tool for judging training effectiveness because of its simplicity, comprehensiveness and business orientation. Its limitations - assumed causality, neglect of context, difficulty and cost of higher levels and absence of a cost dimension - are real but can be managed by planning evaluation during design, supplementing it with process evaluation and ROI, and applying it consistently. Used in this way, it turns training from an act of faith into a measured investment.

## Q56 | 8 | Analyze how the choice among on-the-job, off-the-job, and e-learning methods can affect training outcomes.

### 1. Introduction

Training outcomes - learning, transfer to the job, performance improvement, cost-effectiveness and participant satisfaction - depend not only on content but on the **method** through which learning is delivered. The three broad families of methods are **on-the-job training** (coaching, job instruction, job rotation, apprenticeship, mentoring), **off-the-job training** (lectures, case studies, role plays, simulations, vestibule training, seminars) and **e-learning** (online modules, virtual classrooms, simulations, mobile learning). Each has a distinct profile of strengths and weaknesses, and the match between method, learning objective, learners and context largely determines the result.

### 2. How each method family shapes outcomes

| Outcome dimension | On-the-job | Off-the-job | E-learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type of learning best achieved | Practical, job-specific skills; tacit knowledge | Concepts, principles, analysis, attitudes, interpersonal skills, complex skills practised safely | Knowledge, procedures, compliance, product information; some skills through simulation |
| Transfer to the job | Very high - learning happens in the real context | Moderate to low unless followed up; artificial setting | Moderate; good for knowledge, weaker for behaviour unless blended |
| Consistency and quality | Variable - depends on the supervisor; bad habits may be transmitted | High - expert trainers, structured content | Very high - identical content for all |
| Speed and scale | Slow; one or few learners at a time | Medium; batches of 15-30 | Very fast; thousands simultaneously |
| Cost per learner | Low direct cost, but hidden cost of errors and supervisor time | High - trainers, venue, travel, time away | High development cost, very low delivery cost |
| Learner engagement and interaction | High relevance; limited reflection; production pressure | High interaction with peers and experts; broad perspective | Self-paced but isolated; risk of low completion without motivation |
| Feedback | Immediate, from real results and supervisor | From trainer and peers during exercises | Automated, instant for tests; limited for judgement and behaviour |
| Risk during learning | Errors affect real output, customers or safety | Safe to fail | Safe to fail |
| Measurability | Observed performance | Tests, exercises | Detailed tracking through the LMS |

### 3. Analysis of the effects of the choice

1. **Fit between method and objective is decisive.** Teaching machine operation by lecture (off-the-job) produces knowledge without skill; teaching strategic thinking by on-the-job repetition produces habit without understanding; teaching customer-handling behaviour by e-learning alone produces awareness without practice. A mismatched method lowers learning at Kirkpatrick Level 2 and, more sharply, behaviour at Level 3.
2. **Transfer depends on similarity to the job.** On-the-job methods have the highest transfer because there is no gap between the learning and the working context. Off-the-job and e-learning gain transfer only when they use realistic cases, simulations and action plans and when supervisors reinforce application.
3. **Quality depends on the trainer or the design.** On-the-job outcomes vary with the coaching skill and willingness of supervisors; off-the-job outcomes vary with the trainer's competence; e-learning outcomes vary with instructional design quality and learner motivation. The method choice therefore also decides where investment in quality is needed - train-the-trainer for OJT, expert selection for off-the-job, design and content for e-learning.
4. **Learner characteristics matter.** Experienced adults with clear needs benefit from OJT and case-based off-the-job work; geographically dispersed or shift-based staff benefit from e-learning; learners with low digital literacy or motivation may fail online.
5. **Cost and scale shape what is feasible.** For a compliance topic reaching 5,000 employees, e-learning gives acceptable outcomes at a fraction of the cost; for developing ten future plant heads, off-the-job programmes combined with job rotation and mentoring are worth the expense.
6. **Culture and climate influence effectiveness.** Where the HRD climate is weak and supervisors are not coaches, OJT fails; where openness is low, role plays and T-groups are resisted; where technology is unfamiliar, e-learning is ignored.
7. **Blending usually optimises outcomes.** Because each family covers different outcomes, a blended design - e-learning for concepts, off-the-job workshops for analysis and practice in a safe setting, on-the-job coaching and projects for application - typically produces higher learning, transfer and cost-effectiveness than any single method.

### 4. Illustration

A bank introducing a new loan-appraisal system compared three approaches. Branches trained only through an e-learning module knew the rules but made process errors; branches trained only in a classroom understood the concepts but struggled with the software; branches trained through e-learning plus a half-day vestibule practice session plus two weeks of on-the-job coaching by a trained officer achieved the lowest error rates and fastest processing times.

### 5. Conclusion

The choice among on-the-job, off-the-job and e-learning methods affects what is learnt, how well it transfers, how consistent and scalable the training is and what it costs. On-the-job methods maximise transfer of practical skills, off-the-job methods build concepts and complex or interpersonal skills safely, and e-learning delivers knowledge consistently and economically at scale. Outcomes are best when the method matches the objective, the learners and the context - and, in most cases, when the three are blended.

## Q57 | 8 | State any two reasons for evaluating training at more than one Kirkpatrick level.

### 1. Background

The Kirkpatrick model evaluates training at four levels - Reaction, Learning, Behaviour and Results. In practice many organisations stop at Level 1 (feedback forms) or, at best, Level 2 (tests). Evaluating at more than one level - ideally at all four for important programmes - is strongly recommended. Two principal reasons are explained below, followed by other supporting reasons.

### 2. Reason 1 - A single level gives incomplete and potentially misleading evidence of effectiveness

- Each level answers a **different question**: Did they like it? Did they learn it? Are they using it? Did it make a difference? A good score on one level does not guarantee a good score on the next. Participants may enjoy a programme (high Reaction) and learn nothing; they may learn a great deal (high Learning) yet never apply it on the job; behaviour may change without measurable business results if the wrong behaviours were targeted.
- Research shows only weak correlations between reactions and learning, and between learning and behaviour. Relying on Level 1 alone therefore rewards entertaining programmes rather than effective ones; relying on Level 2 alone certifies knowledge that may never be used.
- Only by measuring at multiple levels can the organisation say with confidence that the training achieved its **purpose - improved job performance and organisational outcomes**, which is what the investment was made for.
- **Example:** A safety programme received excellent ratings and high test scores, yet accident rates did not fall; only Level 3 observation revealed that supervisors were still tolerating short cuts under production pressure.

### 3. Reason 2 - Multiple levels diagnose where and why a programme succeeds or fails, so that the right correction can be made

- Comparing results across levels **locates the break in the chain**:
- Low Reaction and low Learning point to problems of design or delivery - content, methods, trainer, relevance.
- High Learning but low Behaviour points to **transfer problems** in the work environment - no opportunity to apply, unsupportive supervisors, no reinforcement through appraisal and rewards, contradictory systems.
- High Behaviour but weak Results suggests that the training targeted the wrong behaviours or that the performance problem had non-training causes - a needs-assessment failure.
- Each diagnosis calls for a different remedy: redesign the programme, work with line managers and the climate, or redo the TNA. Without multi-level data the organisation cannot tell which remedy is needed and typically blames the trainer or abandons training altogether.
- Multi-level evaluation therefore turns evaluation into a **learning process for the HRD system**, improving the next TNA, design and delivery.

### 4. Other reasons

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Accountability to different stakeholders | Participants care about relevance (Level 1), trainers about learning (Level 2), line managers about behaviour (Level 3) and top management about results and ROI (Level 4); each needs its own evidence |
| Justifying and protecting investment | Only Level 3 and 4 data demonstrate business value and protect training budgets in downturns |
| Strategy alignment | Level 4 measurement forces programmes to be tied to organisational goals |
| Involving line managers | Behaviour and results measurement requires supervisors to observe and support application, strengthening transfer |
| Building an evidence base | Multi-level data across programmes reveal which methods and vendors actually work |
| Compliance and risk | Regulators and auditors increasingly require proof of competence and application, not attendance |

### 5. Conclusion

Training should be evaluated at more than one Kirkpatrick level, first because any single level gives incomplete and possibly misleading evidence - satisfaction is not learning, learning is not application and application is not results - and second because comparing levels diagnoses where a programme breaks down and therefore what must be corrected. Multi-level evaluation converts training from an activity that is merely conducted into an investment that is understood, improved and justified.

## Q58 | 8 | Mention any two factors that should be considered while selecting a training method.

### 1. Why the choice of method matters

A training method is the means through which learning is delivered - lecture, case study, role play, simulation, e-learning, job instruction, coaching, job rotation and so on. The method determines how much is learnt, how well it transfers to the job and what the training costs. The prescribed notes raise the key planning questions - on the job or off the job? in-house or outsourced? on-site or off-site? which methods? Selecting a method is therefore a deliberate decision based on several factors. Two of the most important are explained below, followed by others.

### 2. Factor 1 - The learning objectives (type of learning required)

- The first question is **what participants must be able to do** after training - acquire knowledge, develop a skill or change an attitude - and to what level of proficiency.
- **Knowledge and understanding** (facts, procedures, principles) are efficiently delivered through lectures, reading, e-learning modules and discussions.
- **Motor and technical skills** require demonstration followed by repeated practice with feedback - job instruction training, vestibule training, simulations, coaching.
- **Analytical and decision-making skills** call for case studies, business games, projects and action learning.
- **Interpersonal skills and attitudes** need experiential methods - role plays, behaviour modelling, sensitivity training, group exercises - with feedback and reflection.
- Matching the method to the objective decides whether Level 2 (learning) and Level 3 (behaviour) results will be achieved; a mismatch (for example lecturing about a manual skill) produces knowledge without capability.
- **Example:** To teach nurses a new injection technique, a demonstration and supervised practice on a simulator are chosen rather than a lecture; to teach them the reasons behind the protocol, a short e-learning module is added.

### 3. Factor 2 - Characteristics of the trainees (number, level, experience and learning readiness)

- **Number of trainees:** large numbers spread over locations favour e-learning and lectures; small groups allow case discussion, role play and coaching; single individuals suit mentoring and job instruction.
- **Existing knowledge and experience:** experienced employees respond to problem-centred, participative methods that draw on their experience; beginners need structured, step-by-step instruction.
- **Education, language and digital literacy:** determine whether reading-based or online methods are feasible.
- **Learning styles and preferences:** some learn by doing, some by observing, some by analysing; variety of methods increases effectiveness.
- **Motivation and readiness:** self-paced e-learning requires disciplined, motivated learners; less motivated groups need facilitated, interactive sessions.
- **Level in the organisation:** operatives typically need skill-focused on-the-job methods; managers need conceptual and behavioural methods such as cases, simulations and development programmes.
- **Example:** For 3,000 dispersed field staff with smartphones and varying education, short mobile micro-learning videos with quizzes are chosen over a centralised classroom course.

### 4. Other factors to consider

| Factor | Consideration |
|---|---|
| Cost and budget | Development and delivery costs per learner versus expected benefits; internal versus external resources |
| Time available | Urgency of the need and time trainees can be released from work |
| Trainer competence and availability | Some methods (T-groups, simulations, coaching) need specially skilled facilitators |
| Facilities and technology | Availability of classrooms, equipment, simulators, internet and learning platforms |
| Transfer of learning | How closely the method resembles the job and how easily learning can be applied |
| Content complexity and risk | Dangerous or costly tasks are first learnt in simulation or vestibule settings |
| Organisational culture and climate | Openness to participative and experiential methods; management support for time away from work |
| Learning principles | Opportunity for practice, feedback, active participation, reinforcement and relevance |
| Evaluation requirements | Ease of measuring learning and behaviour with the chosen method |

### 5. Conclusion

Two essential factors in selecting a training method are the learning objectives - the kind and level of knowledge, skill or attitude to be developed - and the characteristics of the trainees - their number, experience, education, readiness and level. Together with cost, time, trainer competence, facilities, transfer requirements and organisational culture, these factors ensure that the method chosen produces real learning that is applied on the job.

## Q59 | 8 | Design a complete training program for a clearly identified employee skill gap, covering TNA, objectives, training method, delivery, and evaluation.

### 1. The identified skill gap

**Organisation:** a mid-sized retail bank with 120 branches.
**Employees:** 240 front-line customer service officers (CSOs).
**Gap:** the bank has introduced a new digital account-opening and KYC (know-your-customer) process. Audit shows that 28 per cent of applications are returned for errors and average processing time is 35 minutes against a standard of 15 minutes. Customer complaints about account opening have doubled. The required competence is "open an account through the new digital process correctly, completely and within 15 minutes, while explaining the process to the customer."

### 2. Stage 1 - Training Needs Assessment

| Level | Findings |
|---|---|
| Organisation analysis | Digital onboarding is a strategic priority; top management supports training; error and complaint data provide a baseline (28 per cent errors, 35 minutes, complaint index 100) |
| Task analysis | Twelve steps in the process; critical KSAs - navigating the new software, document verification rules under KYC, data-entry accuracy, handling exceptions, explaining the process and benefits to customers |
| Person analysis | Tests and observation of a sample of 60 CSOs: 70 per cent unsure of KYC rules for specific document types; 55 per cent make navigation errors; 30 per cent lack confidence in explaining the process; experienced staff perform worse than recent recruits, indicating habit interference |
| Cause analysis | Two non-training causes found - a confusing error message in the software (referred to IT) and no quick-reference guide (to be created as a job aid). The remaining gap is a genuine knowledge and skill deficiency - training is justified |

### 3. Stage 2 - Training goal and learning objectives

**Training goal:** reduce application errors from 28 per cent to below 8 per cent and processing time from 35 to 15 minutes within three months of training, and halve related customer complaints within six months.

**Learning objectives - by the end of the programme each CSO will be able to:**

1. State the KYC documentation rules for all eight customer categories without reference (knowledge - 90 per cent test score).
2. Complete a digital account-opening for a standard customer in the training environment within 15 minutes with zero errors (skill).
3. Correctly resolve the five most common exception cases using the job aid (skill).
4. Explain the process, benefits and timelines to a customer clearly and courteously, as assessed by a role-play checklist (attitude and communication).

### 4. Stage 3 - Training methods (blended design)

| Component | Method | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-work | E-learning module on KYC rules and process overview with quiz (must score 80 per cent to attend the workshop) | 2 hours, self-paced, on the LMS |
| Workshop | Vestibule training in the bank's training environment: demonstration by an expert, guided practice on ten sample cases, exception handling, role plays on customer explanation with peer feedback | 1 day, batches of 16, at regional training centres |
| On-the-job follow-through | Job instruction and coaching by a trained branch "digital champion" for the first 20 live applications; job aid at every desk | 2 weeks |
| Reinforcement | Weekly five-minute micro-learning tips on the mobile app; a virtual clinic (one hour) after three weeks to resolve issues | 4 weeks |

Methods were chosen to match objectives (e-learning for knowledge, vestibule practice and coaching for skills, role play for customer interaction), the number and dispersion of trainees, the need for safe practice before live customer work and cost-effectiveness.

### 5. Stage 4 - Delivery plan

- **Trainers:** two internal operations experts per region trained through a two-day train-the-trainer programme; 120 branch digital champions (one per branch) prepared through a one-day coaching-skills session.
- **Schedule:** 15 workshops over six weeks, sequenced region-wise; participants released on low-footfall days; branch managers briefed in advance on objectives and their role in supporting application.
- **Logistics:** training environment with sample customer data, one terminal per participant, printed job aids, role-play scripts, feedback checklists; LMS enrolment and reminders.
- **Learning climate:** objectives shared at the start; errors during practice treated as learning; individual feedback; action plan completed by each participant at the close.
- **Budget:** trainer time, venue, travel, e-learning development, job aids and participant time itemised and approved.

### 6. Stage 5 - Evaluation plan (Kirkpatrick)

| Level | Measure | Timing | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Reaction | Feedback form on relevance, trainer, practice time, confidence to apply | End of workshop | Average 4.2/5 or above |
| 2 Learning | E-learning quiz; post-workshop knowledge test; timed practical assessment; role-play checklist | Pre-work and end of workshop | 90 per cent knowledge; 100 per cent complete practical within 15 minutes with zero errors |
| 3 Behaviour | Audit of live applications per CSO; champion observation checklist; mystery-customer visits | 4 and 12 weeks after training | Error rate below 8 per cent; processing time 15 minutes; 90 per cent compliance on customer explanation |
| 4 Results | Application error rate, processing time, account-opening complaints, digital onboarding volume, cost per account | 3 and 6 months | Errors below 8 per cent; complaints halved; volume up |

Results are compared with the TNA baseline and with a small group of branches trained later (comparison group). Findings are reviewed by HRD and operations; the programme becomes part of induction for new CSOs and is revised as the software changes.

### 7. Conclusion

This programme follows the complete training cycle: a TNA that quantified the gap, separated training from non-training causes and set a baseline; measurable objectives; a blended design of e-learning, vestibule practice, role play and on-the-job coaching; a delivery plan with prepared trainers, involved branch managers and job aids; and an evaluation plan at all four Kirkpatrick levels. Each stage is linked to the previous one, which is what makes the programme complete and its effectiveness demonstrable.

## Q60 | 8 | Evaluate two alternative training programs for the same organizational need using the Kirkpatrick model and recommend the more effective program.

### 1. The organisational need

A manufacturing company has a high rate of quality defects in its assembly section - 6 per cent against a target of 2 per cent - traced by a Training Needs Assessment to operators' inadequate knowledge of the new quality standard and weak skills in using measuring instruments and completing inspection records. Two training programmes have been proposed for the same 150 operators. The Kirkpatrick model - Reaction, Learning, Behaviour, Results - is used to evaluate the two alternatives and to recommend one.

### 2. The two alternatives

| Feature | Programme A - "Quality Awareness Seminar" | Programme B - "Quality Skills Blended Programme" |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Two-day off-site seminar by an external consultant; lectures, videos, motivational session, group discussion; certificate of participation | E-learning module on the standard (2 hours); one-day workshop with demonstration and hands-on practice on instruments and records in a vestibule area; two weeks of on-the-job coaching by trained line leaders; job aids at each station; weekly quality huddles |
| Participants | All 150 operators in three batches of 50 | 150 operators in batches of 15 for the workshop; coaching in own shift |
| Involvement of supervisors | None | Line leaders trained as coaches; supervisors set quality goals and review records |
| Evaluation planned | Feedback form | Feedback form, pre/post test, practical assessment, on-job observation, defect data |
| Cost | Higher (consultant fees, venue, travel, two days' production loss for all) | Moderate (internal design, shorter time away, coaching within shifts) |

### 3. Evaluation at each Kirkpatrick level

#### Level 1 - Reaction

- **Programme A:** likely very positive - professional speaker, off-site venue, break from routine, motivational content. Ratings may reach 4.5/5.
- **Programme B:** positive but perhaps slightly lower - hands-on practice is demanding, and coaching on the job is less "special." Ratings around 4.2/5, with high scores for relevance.
- **Assessment:** A wins on satisfaction; B wins on perceived relevance. Reaction alone cannot decide effectiveness.

#### Level 2 - Learning

- **Programme A:** builds awareness and general knowledge of the standard; large batches and lecture format limit skill practice; without testing, retention is uncertain. Expected knowledge gain moderate; skill gain minimal.
- **Programme B:** e-learning with quiz ensures knowledge; workshop provides demonstration and repeated practice with feedback; practical assessment certifies the skill. Expected knowledge gain high; skill gain high.
- **Assessment:** B clearly superior, especially for the skill component that the TNA identified as critical.

#### Level 3 - Behaviour

- **Programme A:** no supervisor involvement, no practice on real instruments, no job aids, no follow-up. Operators return to the same environment; transfer is likely to be low. Behaviour change may be visible for a few days and then fade.
- **Programme B:** coaching on the job, job aids, quality goals set by supervisors and weekly huddles create opportunity, support and reinforcement - the conditions for transfer. Behaviour change is likely to be sustained.
- **Assessment:** B decisively superior; A suffers from the classic "positive reaction, no performance change" pattern.

#### Level 4 - Results

- **Programme A:** defect rate likely to improve marginally or not at all, because instrument skills and record-keeping habits are unchanged; the cost of two days' production loss and consultant fees yields little return.
- **Programme B:** with correct knowledge, practised skills and reinforced behaviour, the defect rate can realistically move towards the 2 per cent target, reducing rework, scrap and customer complaints; the moderate cost gives a strong return on investment.
- **Assessment:** B superior on results and on cost-effectiveness.

### 4. Summary scorecard

| Kirkpatrick level | Programme A | Programme B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Reaction | High | Moderately high, high relevance |
| 2 Learning | Moderate knowledge, little skill | High knowledge and skill (assessed) |
| 3 Behaviour | Low - no transfer support | High - coaching, job aids, supervisor reinforcement |
| 4 Results | Minimal, uncertain | Substantial, measurable against baseline |
| Cost and ROI | High cost, low return | Moderate cost, high return |

### 5. Recommendation

**Programme B - the Quality Skills Blended Programme - is recommended.** It matches methods to the objectives identified in the TNA (e-learning for knowledge, hands-on practice for skills, coaching for transfer), involves line supervisors, provides job aids and reinforcement, builds in evaluation at all four levels and costs less. Programme A may be enjoyable and create awareness, but it addresses only the knowledge component, provides no skill practice and no transfer support, and is therefore unlikely to reduce defects.

**Suggested improvements to Programme B:** add a short motivational element on the importance of quality (borrowing A's strength), recognise operators and line leaders who achieve zero-defect weeks, and schedule a refresher e-learning module after six months.

### 6. Conclusion

Applying the Kirkpatrick model shows that a programme's value cannot be judged by how well it is received. Programme A scores well on reaction but weakly on learning, behaviour and results; Programme B scores well on all four levels because it builds skills, supports transfer and measures outcomes. For the organisational need of reducing assembly defects, Programme B is the more effective choice.
