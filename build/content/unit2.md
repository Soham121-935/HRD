# Unit 2 - HRD Systems, Subsystems and Strategic Integration

This unit looks at HRD as a system: its structure and components, the individual subsystems (performance appraisal, potential appraisal, training, career planning, feedback and counselling, rewards, OD), the principles for designing effective HRD practices, how the subsystems depend on one another and why the whole system must be aligned with organisational strategy.

> [key] Names and models to remember: **Pareek and Rao** (integrated HRD system, L&T 1975), **Werner and DeSimone** (four-step HRD process: needs assessment - design - implementation - evaluation), **Kirkpatrick** (four levels of evaluation), **OCTAPACE** (HRD culture), **Standard performance - Actual performance = Gap**.

## Q21 | 8 | Explain the structure of an HRD system and describe its major components.

### 1. Meaning of an HRD system

A **system** is a set of inter-related parts working together for a common purpose. An **HRD system** is the set of inter-linked mechanisms (subsystems), processes and structural arrangements through which an organisation continuously develops the competencies of its people, builds a development-oriented culture and thereby achieves its goals. Pareek and Rao, who designed the first integrated HRD system at Larsen & Toubro in 1975, emphasised that the strength of HRD lies not in any single mechanism but in the way all the mechanisms are **integrated** and supported by a common philosophy.

### 2. Structure of an HRD system

```
                 +------------------------------------------------+
                 |  INPUTS: HRD philosophy, organisational goals, |
                 |  people, budget, top-management commitment     |
                 +------------------------+-----------------------+
                                          v
   +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
   |            HRD SUBSYSTEMS (mechanisms)  -  linked to one another         |
   |  Performance appraisal -> Feedback & counselling -> Training ->          |
   |  Potential appraisal -> Career & succession planning -> Rewards -> OD    |
   +-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                          v
                 +------------------------------------------------+
                 |  PROCESSES / CLIMATE: openness, trust, role     |
                 |  clarity, collaboration, learning orientation   |
                 +------------------------+-----------------------+
                                          v
                 +------------------------------------------------+
                 |  OUTPUTS: competent, committed people; healthy |
                 |  culture; organisational effectiveness         |
                 +--------------------- feedback loop ------------+
```

Structurally, the system has five arrangements (as given in the prescribed notes):

1. **Acknowledging the existence of HRD** - HRD must be recognised as a distinct function with an HRD manager who has access to the CEO, so that development is not buried under routine personnel work.
2. **Establishing priorities and status for the function** - HRD should be placed at a high level and given identity as a major function; its head should be a senior manager. Credibility and usefulness depend on this respectability.
3. **Balancing differentiation and integration** - the HRD department usually covers training and development, personnel administration and industrial relations. These have distinct identities and should be differentiated (different people for OD, training, appraisal), yet integrated through regular coordination so that, for example, the training officer's inputs reach the appraisal process.
4. **Linkage between internal and external systems** - HRD must link with other internal functions (corporate planning, finance, production, marketing) and with external resources (consultants, institutes, professional bodies).
5. **Controlling and monitoring mechanisms** - reviews, feedback and evaluation of every HRD activity so that inputs are available for designing the next cycle.

### 3. Major components (subsystems) of an HRD system

| Component | Purpose | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Performance appraisal | Assess performance against agreed goals and identify development needs | Joint goal setting, self-appraisal, review discussion, development plan |
| Feedback and performance counselling | Help the employee understand strengths and weaknesses and plan improvement | Regular, specific, supportive dialogue between superior and subordinate |
| Potential appraisal | Judge capability for higher or different roles | Assessment centres, psychometric tests, potential ratings separate from performance |
| Career planning and development | Match individual aspirations with organisational opportunities | Career paths, individual development plans, job rotation |
| Training and development | Build knowledge, skills and attitudes for present and future roles | TNA, design, delivery, evaluation; on-the-job, off-the-job, e-learning |
| Succession planning | Ensure ready replacements for key positions | Talent pools, fast-track development |
| Rewards and recognition | Reinforce performance and learning | Monetary and non-monetary rewards linked to performance and competence |
| Employee welfare and quality of work life | Sustain motivation and well-being | Welfare measures, work-life balance, participation |
| Organisation development | Improve processes, teams and culture | Survey feedback, team building, quality circles, change management |
| HRD department and data system | Coordinate, record and analyse | HR information system, training records, HRD audit |

### 4. Supporting elements

Along with the subsystems, an HRD system needs an **HRD philosophy** owned by top management, **line managers** who take responsibility for developing their people, a **supportive climate** (OCTAPACE values) and a **phased implementation** - geographically (unit by unit), vertically (level by level) or functionally (function by function) - so that each part is introduced with proper preparation.

### 5. Conclusion

The structure of an HRD system consists of recognised and senior placement of the function, differentiated yet integrated subsystems, linkages with internal and external systems and built-in monitoring. Its major components - appraisal, feedback and counselling, potential appraisal, career planning, training, succession planning, rewards and OD - work like the organs of one body; each supplies inputs to the others, and together they produce competent people and an effective organisation.

## Q22 | 8 | Explain performance appraisal as an HRD subsystem and state its importance for employee development.

### 1. Meaning

**Performance appraisal** is the systematic and periodic assessment of an employee's performance against agreed standards or objectives, followed by a discussion of the results with the employee. As a traditional personnel activity it was used mainly for control decisions - confirmation, increments, promotion and discipline. As an **HRD subsystem**, its primary purpose is different: it is a tool for **understanding and developing the employee**, identifying strengths and weaknesses, planning improvement and feeding information to other subsystems such as training, counselling, potential appraisal and career planning.

### 2. Performance appraisal as an HRD subsystem - key features

1. **Development is the main objective.** Rao and Abraham found that only about 27 per cent of the organisations they surveyed had development as the main objective of appraisal; an HRD-oriented appraisal changes this priority.
2. **Joint goal setting.** At the beginning of the period the superior and subordinate agree on key result areas (KRAs) and performance standards that are linked to departmental and organisational goals, so that the employee knows exactly what is expected.
3. **Self-appraisal.** The employee first assesses his or her own performance and the factors that helped or hindered it; this makes the process participative and reduces defensiveness.
4. **Performance analysis.** The discussion focuses not only on "how much" but on "why" - which capabilities, resources, systems or behaviours affected results.
5. **Review discussion and feedback.** The appraisal ends in a face-to-face dialogue in which feedback is given, and it leads naturally into performance counselling.
6. **Identification of development needs.** Training needs, job rotation requirements, coaching needs and career aspirations are recorded and passed to the training and career planning subsystems.
7. **Separation from salary decisions.** The developmental discussion is held separately from the reward discussion so that the employee can talk about weaknesses openly.
8. **Multiple inputs.** Modern methods such as 360-degree feedback, behaviourally anchored rating scales, critical incidents and management by objectives are used along with the traditional ranking and grading methods.

### 3. The appraisal cycle

```
 Joint goal setting  -->  Performance during period (with coaching)  -->  Self-appraisal
        ^                                                                     |
        |                                                                     v
 Development plan implemented  <--  Feedback and counselling  <--  Review by superior
        |
        +--> Inputs to training, potential appraisal, career planning, rewards
```

### 4. Importance for employee development

| Importance | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Role clarity | Goal setting removes ambiguity about what the job requires and how success is measured |
| Self-awareness | Self-appraisal and feedback help the employee recognise strengths and weaknesses |
| Identification of training needs | Weaknesses become the basis for need-based training rather than random programmes |
| Motivation | Recognition of good performance and clear improvement targets satisfy needs for achievement and growth |
| Better superior-subordinate relationship | Regular dialogue builds trust, openness and a coaching relationship |
| Foundation for potential appraisal and career planning | Consistent performance data help judge readiness for higher roles and plan careers |
| Fair reward and promotion decisions | Objective data make HR decisions equitable, which strengthens the HRD climate |
| Organisational learning | Aggregated appraisal data reveal systemic problems - poor tools, unclear policies - that need OD interventions |

### 5. Conditions for effectiveness

The appraisal must be based on clear, agreed standards; appraisers must be trained to observe, rate and give feedback; the process must be seen as fair and confidential; and the outcomes must actually be used - if development plans are never acted upon, employees quickly treat the appraisal as a ritual.

### 6. Conclusion

As an HRD subsystem, performance appraisal is not a report card but a developmental conversation supported by data. Through joint goal setting, self-appraisal, feedback and development planning, it gives the employee role clarity, self-awareness and direction, and it supplies the information on which training, counselling, potential appraisal, career planning and rewards depend. That is why it is often called the backbone of the HRD system.

## Q23 | 8 | Describe training as an HRD subsystem and explain how it contributes to employee development.

### 1. Meaning of training

**Training** is a planned effort by the organisation to facilitate employees' learning of job-related knowledge, skills and attitudes (Noe). It aims at improving performance in the present job, while **development** prepares people for future roles and **education** builds general understanding. Together, training, education and development are the three learning activities that Nadler identified as the core of HRD.

Training is the **most widely used HRD mechanism**; in some organisations "HRD" and "training" are even used as synonyms. But within an HRD system training is a subsystem that receives inputs from other subsystems (appraisal, potential appraisal, counselling) and delivers outputs to them (developed employees).

### 2. Training as an HRD subsystem

#### (a) Inputs to training

- **Performance appraisal and performance analysis** identify what the employee cannot do well.
- **Potential appraisal and career planning** identify what the employee must learn for the next role.
- **Counselling** reveals attitudinal and behavioural needs.
- **Organisational strategy and technology changes** create new competency requirements.

#### (b) The training cycle

```
 STAGE 1  Training Needs Assessment  (organisation, task and person analysis)
 STAGE 2  Planning and design        (objectives, content, methods, trainers, budget)
 STAGE 3  Implementation / delivery  (on-the-job, off-the-job, e-learning)
 STAGE 4  Evaluation                 (reaction, learning, behaviour, results)
```

#### (c) Outputs of training

Competent employees, improved performance data for the next appraisal, evidence of learning for potential appraisal and career decisions, and a stronger learning culture.

### 3. Types of training within the subsystem

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Induction / orientation | Familiarise new employees with the organisation, job and culture | Company history, policies, safety, team introductions |
| Job / technical training | Build specific skills for the present job | Machine operation, software, accounting standards |
| Behavioural training | Improve attitudes, communication and interpersonal skills | Team work, customer handling, conflict management |
| Supervisory and management development | Prepare people for leadership roles | Delegation, decision making, performance counselling |
| Refresher and re-skilling | Update obsolete skills | New technology, new regulations |
| Cross-functional / multi-skilling | Build flexibility and understanding of the whole system | Job rotation, cross-training |

### 4. How training contributes to employee development

1. **Removes skill deficiencies** and raises current job performance, which builds confidence and self-esteem.
2. **Prepares for future roles** - management development and multi-skilling make promotions and transfers possible.
3. **Develops attitudes and behaviour** - behavioural training improves teamwork, communication and customer orientation.
4. **Improves employability and adaptability** - updated skills protect the employee from obsolescence and make change less frightening.
5. **Increases motivation and satisfaction** - investment in training shows that the organisation values the person; it satisfies growth needs and reduces turnover.
6. **Supports career planning** - each training input is a step on the individual development plan.
7. **Builds a learning habit** - regular training creates a culture in which continuous learning is normal.
8. **Generates organisational benefits** that come back to employees - higher productivity, fewer accidents, better quality, a stronger company image and more opportunities.

### 5. Conditions for training to actually develop people

Studies of Indian organisations (Rao and Abraham; Jain; D'Souza) found that training often gives a poor return because employees are sent as a "fringe benefit," needs are not analysed, and there is little opportunity to try out what was learnt. For training to contribute to development, therefore, it must be **need-based**, linked to appraisal and career plans, supported by supervisors who allow application of the new skills (transfer of training), and **evaluated** at the levels of behaviour and results, not just reaction.

### 6. Conclusion

Training is the action arm of the HRD system: it converts the needs identified by appraisal, potential appraisal and counselling into actual capability. When it is need-based, integrated with the other subsystems and followed by application and evaluation, training develops employees in skills, attitudes and confidence, and prepares them for both present and future roles.

## Q24 | 8 | Explain career planning and potential appraisal as HRD subsystems.

### 1. Introduction

Two HRD subsystems look explicitly at the **future** of the employee. **Potential appraisal** asks "What could this person do in the future?" and **career planning** asks "Where should this person go, and how do we get there?" The two are closely linked: a meaningful career planning system must be based on potential appraisal, because past performance alone is not a reliable indicator of future potential.

### 2. Potential appraisal

#### Meaning

Potential appraisal is the systematic assessment of an employee's **capability to perform higher-level or different roles** in the future - roles that may require competencies not fully used in the present job. It is different from performance appraisal, which looks at results already achieved in the current role.

#### Features

- Focuses on latent abilities - conceptual thinking, leadership, decision making, initiative, learning ability - rather than on current output.
- Uses multiple methods: assessment centres (in-basket exercises, group discussions, business simulations, role plays), psychometric and aptitude tests, structured interviews, supervisor ratings on potential attributes and a review of performance history.
- Is conducted against defined **role requirements** of the target positions, so the organisation must first describe the competencies needed at higher levels.
- Results are shared with the employee and used for development, placement and succession decisions.

#### Steps

1. Identify the roles and the competencies required for them.
2. Choose assessment mechanisms for each competency.
3. Assess the employee through the chosen mechanisms and multiple assessors.
4. Communicate the results and prepare a development plan.
5. Link the results to career planning, succession planning and training.

#### Status in India

Pareek and Rao observed that most Indian organisations collect potential data on the performance appraisal form, which mixes the two purposes. Systematic potential appraisal is still limited to larger companies, though the use of assessment centres is growing.

### 3. Career planning

#### Meaning

Career planning is the systematic process by which an individual selects career goals and the path to those goals, and by which the organisation helps employees plan their careers in line with its own future needs. A career plan is a blueprint mapping the employee's growth from entry to retirement.

#### Characteristics

- It is a **process**, not an event; it is continuous because both the person and the environment keep changing.
- It is primarily the **individual's responsibility**, but the organisation must provide guidance, information and counselling.
- Its basic aim is the **integration of individual and organisational needs**.

#### Process

```
 Identify individual needs and aspirations --> Analyse career opportunities in the organisation
        --> Identify match and mismatch --> Formulate and implement strategies
        (training, rotation, mentoring, projects) --> Review career plans periodically
```

#### Objectives

To attract and retain the right people; to map careers suited to ability and willingness to be developed; to ensure better use of human resources through satisfied and productive employees; to build a stable workforce with lower turnover; and to improve morale by helping employees fulfil their potential.

### 4. How the two subsystems work together

| Aspect | Potential appraisal | Career planning |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | How far and in what direction can the person grow? | What path should the person follow to grow? |
| Time focus | Future capability | Future movement |
| Input from | Performance appraisal, assessment centres, tests | Potential appraisal, individual aspirations, manpower plan |
| Output to | Career planning, succession planning, training | Training, job rotation, succession planning, rewards |
| Benefit to employee | Knows strengths and development areas | Sees a clear, realistic growth path |
| Benefit to organisation | Identifies talent pool early | Retains talent and fills positions internally |

Potential appraisal supplies the objective assessment on which realistic career plans are built; career planning gives the potential appraisal results a practical direction. Together they feed **succession planning**, which ensures that key positions always have ready replacements.

### 5. Example

A bank assesses its young officers through an assessment centre and identifies those with high potential for branch leadership. For each, a career plan is prepared - credit exposure in year 2, a rural branch posting in year 3, a leadership programme in year 4 and a deputy branch manager role in year 5 - with mentoring and reviews along the way.

### 6. Conclusion

Potential appraisal identifies what a person could become; career planning charts how the person will get there. As HRD subsystems they turn the organisation's attention from yesterday's results to tomorrow's capability, motivate employees with a visible future and give the organisation a dependable internal supply of talent.

## Q25 | 8 | Explain the role of feedback and counselling in an HRD system.

### 1. Meaning

**Feedback** is information given to an employee about his or her performance and behaviour - what was done well, what fell short and what effect it had. **Performance counselling** is the purposeful, confidential conversation between a superior (or a trained counsellor) and an employee in which feedback is discussed, causes are explored and the employee is helped to plan improvement and development. Counselling may also address emotional and personal problems - job dissatisfaction, stress, conflict, resistance to change - that affect work.

In an HRD system, feedback and counselling form the **bridge between appraisal and development**. Appraisal produces data; feedback communicates it; counselling converts it into insight and an action plan; training, career planning and rewards then act on the plan.

### 2. Purposes of feedback and counselling in HRD

1. **To help employees know where they stand** - reducing ambiguity and anxiety about performance.
2. **To recognise strengths and weaknesses** so that development is targeted.
3. **To identify causes of performance problems** - skill, knowledge, attitude, resources or systems - and choose the right remedy.
4. **To build commitment to improvement** through joint problem solving rather than instructions.
5. **To strengthen the superior-subordinate relationship** on the basis of trust, openness and mutual respect.
6. **To release emotional tension**, reassure, clarify thinking and reorient the employee (the classic counselling functions of advice, reassurance, communication, release of tension, clarified thinking and reorientation).
7. **To support career development** - discussing aspirations and realistic paths.
8. **To reinforce the HRD climate** - regular, supportive feedback is one of the strongest signals that the organisation cares about development.

### 3. The counselling process

```
 Step 1  Describe the observed behaviour or performance (specific facts, not personality)
 Step 2  Invite the employee's view and explore reasons  (listen, empathise)
 Step 3  Agree on a solution and development actions   (emphasise confidentiality)
 Step 4  Summarise and obtain commitment to change
 Step 5  Follow up and review progress
```

Eisenberg and Delaney list the aims of counselling as helping people understand themselves, make sound decisions, set achievable growth goals, plan the future, solve personal and interpersonal problems, cope with difficult situations, control self-defeating emotions and acquire positive self-regard.

### 4. Characteristics of effective feedback

| Effective feedback is... | Ineffective feedback is... |
|---|---|
| Specific and descriptive - based on observed behaviour and data | Vague and evaluative - "you have a bad attitude" |
| Timely - given soon after the event and regularly | Saved up and delivered once a year |
| Balanced - covers strengths as well as areas for improvement | Only negative, given when something goes wrong |
| Focused on things the person can change | Focused on personality or things beyond control |
| Two-way - invites the employee's perspective | One-way lecture |
| Followed by support - training, coaching, resources | Followed by nothing |

### 5. Role within the HRD system

- **From appraisal:** feedback and counselling are the natural conclusion of the appraisal cycle.
- **To training:** counselling identifies genuine training needs and motivates the employee to learn.
- **To potential appraisal and career planning:** discussion of aspirations and capabilities feeds career plans.
- **To rewards:** recognition given during feedback is itself a non-monetary reward.
- **To OD:** patterns emerging across many counselling sessions reveal systemic issues - work overload, unclear roles, poor tools - that need organisational intervention.

### 6. Practice in Indian organisations

Rao and Abraham found that about 80 per cent of surveyed companies required executives to counsel subordinates, but in most cases feedback was given in a threatening way or as a warning rather than as development. Giving warnings and enforcing norms are part of a manager's job, but counselling in the HRD sense helps the employee recognise potential and prepares him or her for self-development. Training managers in counselling skills is therefore essential.

### 7. Conclusion

Feedback and counselling are the human, dialogic core of the HRD system. They convert appraisal data into self-awareness, commitment and action plans, strengthen the relationship between superiors and subordinates, and feed every other subsystem. Without them, appraisal becomes a form, training becomes a guess and career planning becomes wishful thinking.

## Q26 | 8 | Describe the major principles for designing effective HRD practices.

### 1. Introduction

HRD systems must be designed differently for different organisations. The basic principles remain the same, but the specific components, their relationships, phasing and processes vary with the organisation's culture, size, technology and existing practices. Designing an integrated HRD system therefore requires a thorough understanding of the principles of HRD and a diagnosis of the organisation's culture, existing practices, employee perceptions and developmental climate. The principles below are drawn from the classic work of Pareek and Rao and from the prescribed notes on HRD system design.

### 2. Principles relating to focus

1. **Focus on enabling capabilities.** The primary purpose of HRD is to increase the organisation's "enabling" capabilities - development of human resources, organisational health, problem-solving capability, diagnostic ability and employee productivity and commitment - not merely to run programmes.
2. **Organisational strategy alignment.** The HRD system should be tightly aligned with the strategic aims of the organisation and should build the talents, knowledge and abilities that those aims require.
3. **Needs-driven approach.** A thorough analysis of the present and future needs of the organisation, teams and individuals should be the basis of design; needs decide priorities and objectives.
4. **Balancing adaptation and change in the culture.** HRD systems should suit the culture, yet HRD's role is also to modify the culture to increase effectiveness. The design should anticipate change and take the organisation forward.
5. **Attention to contextual factors.** What is included, how it is sub-divided and even the titles used should depend on culture, tradition, size, technology, existing skill levels and available support.

### 3. Principles relating to structure

6. **Building linkages with other functions.** HRD should strengthen corporate planning, budgeting, finance, marketing and production; these linkages are extremely important.
7. **Balancing specialisation and diffusion of the function.** HRD involves specialised skills, but line people must be involved; action is the responsibility of line managers, and HRD should strengthen their roles.
8. **Ensuring respectability for the function.** HRD should be instituted at a high level, with a senior manager as head, because its credibility and usefulness depend on this.
9. **Balancing differentiation and integration.** Personnel administration, HRD/training and industrial relations have distinct identities and should be differentiated within the department, yet integrated through coordination so that inputs flow between them.
10. **Establishing linkage mechanisms.** Formal mechanisms - task forces, committees, regular reviews - should connect HRD with line managers and with other HR functions.

### 4. Principles relating to functioning

11. **Integration and coherence.** The subsystems - appraisal, training, career planning, counselling, rewards, OD - must support and reinforce one another rather than run as separate activities.
12. **Developing monitoring mechanisms and reviews.** Feedback on each programme, matching qualitative and quantitative judgements, and periodic HRD audit keep the system healthy.
13. **Balancing internal and external resources.** Develop internal experts for cost-effectiveness and continuity, and use external experts where special knowledge is required.
14. **Planning the growth of the function (phasing).** Introduce the system in phases - geographical (unit by unit), vertical (level by level) or functional (function by function) - with proper preparation rather than rushing.
15. **Customisation and stakeholder involvement.** Involve employees, managers and leaders in the design, and personalise learning so that it is relevant and owned.
16. **Scalability and flexibility.** The system should grow or shrink with the organisation and respond to changes in strategy.
17. **Continuous learning and improvement, integration of technology, accountability and measurement, and sustainability** - a culture of ongoing learning, use of technology (LMS, HRIS, e-learning), KPIs to measure results and return on investment, and resources planned for the long term.

### 5. Summary table

| Group | Principles |
|---|---|
| Focus | Enabling capabilities; strategy alignment; needs-driven; balance adaptation and change; contextual factors |
| Structure | Linkages with other functions; specialisation vs diffusion; respectability; differentiation vs integration; linkage mechanisms |
| Functioning | Integration; monitoring and review; internal-external balance; phased growth; stakeholder involvement; flexibility; technology; measurement; sustainability |

### 6. Conclusion

Effective HRD practices are designed on principles rather than copied from other organisations. They focus on enabling capabilities aligned with strategy and based on real needs; they are structured so that HRD is respected, differentiated yet integrated and linked with line functions; and they function as a coherent, monitored, phased and continuously improving system. Applying these principles is what turns a collection of HR activities into a genuine HRD system.

## Q27 | 8 | Explain how the major HRD subsystems are interrelated within an organization.

### 1. Introduction

The subsystems of HRD - performance appraisal, feedback and counselling, potential appraisal, career planning, training and development, succession planning, rewards and organisation development - are not independent activities. Each one produces information or capability that the others need, and each depends on the others to make its own output useful. Pareek and Rao described this as an **integrated HRD system** in which the mechanisms reinforce one another; when one is missing or weak, the effectiveness of all the others falls.

### 2. The web of interrelationships

```
                          ORGANISATIONAL GOALS AND STRATEGY
                                        |
                                        v
      +----------------->  PERFORMANCE APPRAISAL  <------------------+
      |                  (goals, results, gaps)                      |
      |                          |                                   |
      |                          v                                   |
      |               FEEDBACK AND COUNSELLING                       |
      |         (insight, causes, development plan)                  |
      |            /            |             \                      |
      |           v             v              v                     |
   REWARDS    TRAINING     POTENTIAL       CAREER PLANNING           |
  (reinforce) (build      APPRAISAL   <-->  (path, IDP)  --> SUCCESSION PLANNING
      ^        skills)    (future            |                       |
      |           |        capability)       |                       |
      +-----------+-------------+------------+-----------------------+
                                |
                                v
                     ORGANISATION DEVELOPMENT
              (culture, teams, systems that support all of the above)
```

### 3. Specific linkages explained

| Linkage | How the subsystems depend on each other |
|---|---|
| Appraisal -> Feedback and counselling | Appraisal produces data on performance; counselling communicates it and turns it into a development plan. Without counselling, appraisal is a report card; without appraisal, counselling has no facts |
| Appraisal and counselling -> Training | Training needs are identified from appraisal, performance analysis and counselling. Training that is not based on these becomes a "fringe benefit" with poor return |
| Training -> Appraisal | The next appraisal measures whether training changed performance; this evaluates the training and identifies further needs |
| Appraisal -> Potential appraisal | Consistent performance data are one input for judging potential, though potential also needs separate assessment of future-oriented competencies |
| Potential appraisal -> Career planning | Career plans are realistic only when based on assessed potential; otherwise they become wish lists |
| Career planning -> Training and job rotation | The individual development plan prescribes the training, projects and rotations needed to move along the career path |
| Potential appraisal and career planning -> Succession planning | High-potential employees with defined career paths form the talent pool for key positions |
| Appraisal -> Rewards | Rewards linked to appraised performance and demonstrated competence reinforce the right behaviour and make appraisal credible |
| Rewards -> Motivation to learn | Recognition of learning and improvement motivates employees to use training and act on feedback |
| All subsystems -> Organisation development | Aggregated appraisal, counselling and survey data reveal systemic problems; OD interventions (team building, role clarification, culture change) create the climate in which the other subsystems can work |
| OD and HRD climate -> All subsystems | Openness and trust are preconditions for honest self-appraisal, candid counselling and willingness to experiment with new skills |

### 4. Example of the chain in action

An engineer's appraisal shows excellent technical results but weak coordination with other departments. In the counselling session the cause emerges - lack of exposure to project management. Training in project management is arranged, and a cross-functional project is assigned (job rotation). The potential appraisal a year later shows leadership promise, so the career plan marks her for a project-lead role, the succession plan lists her as a future department head, and the reward system recognises her improved coordination. Each subsystem used the output of the previous one.

### 5. Consequences of breaking the links

If appraisal is not followed by counselling, employees do not know what to improve. If training is chosen without appraisal data, it is irrelevant. If potential appraisal results do not lead to career plans, high-potential people leave. If rewards ignore appraisal, the appraisal loses credibility. Poor coordination among subsystems is therefore one of the main reasons why HRD investments fail to improve performance.

### 6. Conclusion

The HRD subsystems are interrelated like links in a chain and like organs in a body: appraisal generates data, counselling creates insight, training builds capability, potential appraisal and career planning give direction, succession planning secures the future, rewards reinforce and OD provides the healthy environment. Their strength lies in their integration - designed together, sequenced sensibly and continuously exchanging information.

## Q28 | 8 | Explain the need for integrating HRD practices with organizational strategy.

### 1. Meaning of integration with strategy

**Organisational strategy** is the long-term plan by which an organisation intends to achieve its goals - for example cost leadership, quality differentiation, innovation, diversification, digital transformation or geographic expansion. **Integrating HRD with strategy** means deriving HRD goals from the strategy, building the specific competencies and culture that the strategy requires, and involving HRD in the formulation of strategy so that people considerations shape the plan. The first principle of HRD system design in the prescribed notes is precisely **organisational strategy alignment**.

### 2. Why integration is necessary

1. **Strategy is executed by people.** A strategy is only a document until employees have the knowledge, skills and attitudes to implement it. A quality strategy needs statistical and problem-solving skills; a customer-intimacy strategy needs service and relationship skills; a digital strategy needs technology skills. HRD is the function that builds these.
2. **Competency building takes time.** Skills, leadership and culture cannot be bought overnight. Only HRD that knows the strategy in advance can develop, say, forty plant managers for expansion three years ahead.
3. **Avoiding wasted investment.** Training that is not linked to strategy produces programmes nobody needs; Indian studies (D'Souza; Rao and Abraham) found training treated as a "fringe benefit" with poor return. Alignment ensures that every rupee spent on development contributes to a business goal.
4. **Coherence of HRD subsystems.** Strategy provides the common reference point that aligns appraisal goals, training priorities, potential criteria, career paths and rewards. Without it, the subsystems pull in different directions.
5. **Culture as strategic capability.** Strategies such as innovation or customer focus require corresponding cultures - experimentation, openness, collaboration. HRD's OD role shapes culture, and it must know which culture the strategy demands.
6. **Managing change.** Strategic shifts - mergers, new technology, restructuring - succeed only when people are prepared; HRD integrated with strategy plans this preparation instead of reacting to resistance later.
7. **Competitive advantage and sustainability.** People-based capabilities are hard for competitors to imitate. HRD aligned with strategy converts human potential into a lasting advantage.
8. **Credibility and resources for HRD.** When HRD can show its contribution to strategic goals in business terms, it earns a seat at the top table and stable budgets.
9. **Stage of organisational life cycle.** The HRD goals of an embryonic, high-growth, mature or ageing company differ; strategy tells HRD which stage the organisation is in and what it therefore needs - strong managers, intellectual capital, disciplined execution or revitalisation.

### 3. How the linkage works

```
 BUSINESS STRATEGY --> Capability and culture requirements --> HRD STRATEGY AND GOALS
      ^                                                              |
      |                                                              v
 People capabilities and constraints inform strategy   <--   HRD PRACTICES: appraisal
 (HRD as strategic advisor)                                  goals, training priorities,
                                                             potential criteria, career
                                                             paths, rewards, OD
                                                                     |
                                                                     v
                                                           MEASURED BUSINESS RESULTS
```

### 4. Illustration

| Strategy | What HRD must build |
|---|---|
| Cost leadership | Multi-skilling, process discipline, waste-reduction skills, productivity-linked rewards |
| Differentiation through quality | Quality tools, problem solving, quality circles, customer-complaint analysis |
| Innovation | Creativity techniques, experimentation culture, cross-functional teams, tolerance for failure |
| Rapid expansion | Leadership pipeline, fast-track development, standardised induction |
| Digital transformation | Technology re-skilling, e-learning platforms, change management |

### 5. Consequences of non-integration

Skill shortages at critical moments, training budgets spent on irrelevant programmes, appraisal goals disconnected from business priorities, high-potential employees developed for roles that no longer exist, cultural resistance to strategic change and, finally, loss of credibility for the HRD function.

### 6. Conclusion

HRD practices must be integrated with organisational strategy because strategy is executed through people, capability takes time to build, resources must not be wasted and the HRD subsystems need a common direction. When HRD is derived from and feeds into strategy, it stops being a support activity and becomes the mechanism through which the organisation's plans become reality.

## Q29 | 8 | An organization has an appraisal system but employees report that it does not help their development. Apply HRD principles to improve the appraisal process.

### 1. Diagnosis of the problem

An appraisal that "does not help development" usually shows some of these symptoms: it is used only to decide increments and promotions; goals were never agreed at the start; the employee is rated once a year without discussion; ratings are subjective and appraisers are untrained; there is no self-appraisal; no feedback or counselling follows; identified needs are never converted into training or career action; and results are kept secret or used punitively. In HRD terms, the appraisal exists as a **control mechanism**, not as a **development subsystem**, and it is not integrated with the other subsystems.

### 2. HRD principles to be applied

| HRD principle | Application to the appraisal process |
|---|---|
| Development as primary objective | Redefine the purpose: appraisal is first a tool for understanding and developing the employee; reward decisions are a secondary use |
| Needs-driven and enabling capabilities | Use appraisal to identify capability gaps and development needs, not merely to rank people |
| Participation and openness (OCTAPACE) | Introduce joint goal setting and self-appraisal; make the process a dialogue |
| Integration and coherence | Link appraisal outputs to counselling, training, potential appraisal, career planning and rewards |
| Line manager involvement | Make appraisers responsible and competent for developing subordinates; train them |
| Strategy alignment | Derive individual KRAs from departmental and organisational goals |
| Monitoring and review | Audit the quality of appraisals and act on employee feedback about the system |
| Trust and confidentiality | Keep developmental discussions confidential and separate from salary discussions |

### 3. Recommended redesign of the appraisal process

#### Step 1 - Clarify purpose and gain commitment

Top management should state that the appraisal is primarily developmental, and managers' own appraisals should include "development of subordinates" as a KRA.

#### Step 2 - Joint goal setting at the beginning of the period

Superior and subordinate agree on 4-6 KRAs with measurable standards derived from the unit's goals. The employee knows what is expected and how it will be measured.

#### Step 3 - Continuous coaching and a mid-year review

Feedback should not wait for the year end. A mid-year review corrects course while there is still time.

#### Step 4 - Self-appraisal

The employee first records achievements, difficulties, facilitating and hindering factors, and development needs. This reduces defensiveness and gives the appraiser the employee's perspective.

#### Step 5 - Performance analysis, not just rating

Discuss the reasons for results - skill, knowledge, attitude, resources, systems - so that the right remedy (training or non-training) is chosen.

#### Step 6 - Feedback and performance counselling session

A structured conversation: describe behaviour with facts, listen to the employee, agree on solutions, obtain commitment, plan follow-up. Train all appraisers in feedback and counselling skills.

#### Step 7 - Individual development plan (IDP)

Every appraisal ends with a written IDP - training, job rotation, projects, mentoring, reading - with time lines and responsibilities. The HRD department consolidates IDPs into the training plan.

#### Step 8 - Separate the development review from the reward review

Hold the developmental discussion first and the increment/promotion discussion later, so that employees can speak openly about weaknesses.

#### Step 9 - Improve the instrument and reduce bias

Use behaviourally anchored scales, critical incidents and, where the climate permits, multi-source (360-degree) inputs. Calibrate ratings across appraisers to ensure fairness.

#### Step 10 - Link to other subsystems and review the system

Feed appraisal data into potential appraisal and career planning; recognise improvement through rewards; conduct an annual survey of employees on appraisal usefulness and audit a sample of forms for quality.

### 4. Expected results

Employees gain role clarity and self-awareness; training becomes need-based; superiors become coaches; high performers see development leading somewhere; trust in the system rises; and the organisation obtains reliable data for HR decisions.

### 5. Conclusion

Applying HRD principles converts the appraisal from a yearly rating exercise into a developmental cycle of goal setting, coaching, self-appraisal, analysis, counselling and action planning that is integrated with training, career planning and rewards. When employees experience these changes, they will report that the appraisal helps their development - which is the real test of an HRD-oriented appraisal.

### 1. The situation

An employee - say a senior engineer or assistant manager with four to five years of experience - has been identified through potential appraisal as having leadership potential. The task is to design a development path that converts potential into demonstrated leadership capability, using the HRD subsystems in an integrated manner. The path should be planned over roughly three years and reviewed regularly.

Integrated in this way, the subsystems convert identified potential into a ready leader while keeping the employee motivated and committed to the organisation.

## Q31 | 8 | State any two HRD subsystems.

### 1. Meaning of an HRD subsystem

An HRD system is made up of several inter-related mechanisms, each of which performs a specific developmental function and supplies inputs to the others. These mechanisms are called **HRD subsystems** (or HRD mechanisms / instruments). Pareek and Rao listed the main subsystems practised in Indian organisations as performance appraisal, potential appraisal, feedback and performance counselling, career planning and development, training, rewards, employee welfare and quality of work life, and organisation development. Any two can be stated; two are explained in detail below and the rest are summarised.

### 2. Subsystem 1 - Performance appraisal

- **Meaning:** The systematic, periodic assessment of an employee's performance against agreed goals and standards, followed by a discussion of the results with the employee.
- **Developmental purpose:** As an HRD subsystem it is used mainly to help the employee understand strengths and weaknesses, plan improvement and identify training and career needs - not merely to decide increments.
- **Key features:** joint goal setting (KRAs linked to organisational goals), self-appraisal, performance analysis of helping and hindering factors, review discussion, and a development plan.
- **Links with other subsystems:** its output goes to feedback and counselling, training, potential appraisal, career planning and rewards; it is therefore called the backbone of the HRD system.
- **Example:** A sales officer and her manager agree on targets and development goals in April, review progress in October, and in March discuss results and agree that she will attend a key-account management programme.

### 3. Subsystem 2 - Training and development

- **Meaning:** A planned effort by the organisation to help employees learn job-related knowledge, skills and attitudes (training) and to prepare them for future roles (development).
- **Developmental purpose:** It removes skill deficiencies, prepares people for higher responsibilities, improves attitudes and behaviour, and keeps competencies current in the face of technological change.
- **Key features:** it follows a four-stage cycle - training needs assessment, design, implementation and evaluation - and uses on-the-job methods (coaching, job rotation, apprenticeship), off-the-job methods (lectures, case studies, role plays, simulations) and e-learning.
- **Links with other subsystems:** training needs come from appraisal, counselling and potential appraisal; trained employees perform better in the next appraisal and progress along their career paths.
- **Example:** Appraisals across a plant reveal weak knowledge of a new quality standard; the HRD department designs a two-day workshop followed by on-the-job coaching and evaluates the reduction in defects three months later.

### 4. Other HRD subsystems (for completeness)

| Subsystem | Purpose in one line |
|---|---|
| Potential appraisal | Assess capability for higher or different future roles |
| Feedback and performance counselling | Communicate appraisal results and help the employee plan improvement |
| Career planning and development | Match individual aspirations with organisational opportunities through defined career paths |
| Succession planning | Prepare ready replacements for key positions |
| Rewards and recognition | Reinforce good performance and learning through monetary and non-monetary rewards |
| Employee welfare and quality of work life | Sustain well-being and motivation |
| Organisation development | Improve culture, teams and processes through planned interventions |
| HRD department / information system | Coordinate the subsystems and maintain data |

### 5. Conclusion

Performance appraisal and training are two of the most important HRD subsystems - the first diagnoses and directs development, the second builds the required capability. Like the other subsystems, they are most effective when they are integrated, so that the output of one becomes the input of the next.

## Q32 | 8 | State any two purposes of feedback in HRD practices.

### 1. Meaning of feedback in HRD

Feedback is information given to an employee about his or her performance and behaviour - what was done well, what fell short of expectations and what impact it had - with the intention of helping the person learn and improve. In HRD practice feedback is the link between assessment (appraisal, potential appraisal, training evaluation) and action (counselling, training, career planning). Two principal purposes are explained below, followed by other purposes.

### 2. Purpose 1 - To help the employee know where he or she stands and to identify development needs

- Employees cannot improve what they do not know is wrong, nor build on strengths they are unaware of. Feedback provides an accurate picture of performance against expectations and thereby **reduces ambiguity and anxiety**.
- By pointing out specific strengths and specific gaps, feedback converts a general appraisal rating into **concrete development needs** - a skill to learn, a behaviour to change, an exposure to seek.
- This purpose makes feedback the foundation for need-based training and for the individual development plan; without it, training is chosen by guesswork.
- **Example:** Feedback that "your reports are technically correct but arrive two days late and are hard to read" tells the employee exactly what to develop - time management and written communication - whereas a rating of "3 out of 5" tells nothing.

### 3. Purpose 2 - To motivate, reinforce desired behaviour and build commitment to improvement

- Positive feedback recognises effort and achievement; recognition is one of the strongest non-monetary motivators and satisfies higher-order needs for esteem and growth.
- Constructive feedback, given supportively, shows that the superior cares about the employee's growth and invites joint problem solving; this creates **commitment** to the improvement plan rather than mere compliance.
- Regular feedback **reinforces the behaviour the organisation wants** - customer focus, teamwork, safety - and discourages what it does not want, before small problems become large.
- **Example:** A supervisor who notices and appreciates a technician's new safety practice in front of the team makes it more likely that the practice continues and spreads.

### 4. Other purposes of feedback in HRD practices

| Purpose | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Improve superior-subordinate relationship | Regular, honest dialogue builds trust and openness (OCTAPACE values) |
| Support performance counselling | Feedback is the first step of the counselling process - describe the behaviour, then explore reasons and agree on solutions |
| Aid career planning | Feedback on potential and aspirations helps set realistic career goals |
| Evaluate training | Feedback from trainees and from supervisors tells whether learning was transferred to the job |
| Correct performance in time | Mid-year and continuous feedback allow course correction before the annual review |
| Reduce grievances and surprises | Employees who receive feedback throughout the year are not shocked by year-end ratings |
| Build a learning culture | An organisation where feedback flows freely - downward, upward and across - learns faster |

### 5. Characteristics that make feedback serve these purposes

Feedback should be specific and descriptive, timely, balanced between strengths and improvement areas, focused on behaviour the person can change, delivered privately and with respect, two-way, and followed by support such as coaching or training.

### 6. Conclusion

Two central purposes of feedback in HRD are to help employees know where they stand and what to develop, and to motivate them by reinforcing desired behaviour and building commitment to improvement. Because it connects appraisal with counselling, training and career planning, feedback is indispensable to every HRD practice.

## Q33 | 8 | Analyze how poor coordination among HRD subsystems can reduce the effectiveness of employee development.

### 1. Introduction

The HRD subsystems - performance appraisal, feedback and counselling, potential appraisal, career planning, training, succession planning, rewards and OD - are designed to work as an integrated whole; the principle of **integration and coherence** requires that they support and reinforce one another. Poor coordination occurs when these subsystems are designed by different people at different times, run on different calendars, use different criteria and do not exchange information. The analysis below traces how such fragmentation weakens employee development at each link of the chain.

### 2. Where coordination breaks down and what happens

| Broken link | Consequence for employee development |
|---|---|
| Appraisal not followed by feedback and counselling | Employees receive a rating but no explanation; they do not know what to improve; appraisal becomes a ritual and resentment grows |
| Training not based on appraisal or needs analysis | Programmes are chosen from catalogues or given as rewards; content is irrelevant; employees learn little and apply less; training budgets are wasted (the "fringe benefit" problem noted by D'Souza and by Rao and Abraham) |
| Training not reinforced by supervisors and appraisal | Even good learning is not transferred to the job because nobody expects or measures the new behaviour |
| Potential appraisal mixed with performance appraisal | Good performers are assumed to have potential; genuinely high-potential people in ordinary jobs are missed; wrong people are promoted |
| Potential appraisal results not used in career planning | High-potential employees see no future and leave; the talent pool is wasted |
| Career plans not linked to training and job rotation | Career paths remain paper promises; employees are not prepared for the next role when it opens |
| Succession planning disconnected from potential and career data | Key positions are filled by outsiders or by seniority; internal development loses credibility |
| Rewards not linked to appraised performance and learning | Employees see no benefit in improving; appraisal and training lose motivational power; cynicism spreads |
| OD and climate-building ignored | Low trust and fear undermine honest self-appraisal, candid counselling and willingness to experiment with new skills |
| Different criteria across subsystems | An employee is rated "excellent" in appraisal, "average" in potential, receives no training and no increment - contradictory signals destroy faith in the system |

### 3. Cumulative effects on development effectiveness

1. **Learning without application** - skills are acquired in training but never used, so they decay.
2. **Development without direction** - employees attend programmes but do not know how they connect to their career.
3. **Assessment without action** - data are collected in appraisal and potential reviews but nothing happens, which teaches employees that the system is a formality.
4. **Motivation without means** - employees who want to grow do not get the training, rotation or mentoring they need.
5. **Duplication and gaps** - some competencies are trained repeatedly while others are never addressed.
6. **Inequity** - inconsistent decisions across subsystems are perceived as unfair, damaging the HRD climate.
7. **Attrition of talent** - the best people, who value development most, leave first.
8. **Loss of credibility and resources for HRD** - management sees no results and cuts budgets, starting a downward spiral.

### 4. Illustration

A company sends its managers to an expensive leadership programme (training) but its appraisal still measures only sales volume, its rewards pay only for volume and its succession plan is decided informally by the chairman. The managers return, find that leadership behaviour is neither expected nor rewarded, and go back to old habits within a month. The training was good; the coordination was absent.

### 5. How to restore coordination

Design the subsystems together on a common competency framework; run them on one annual calendar (goal setting - mid-year review - appraisal - counselling - IDP - training plan - potential review - career and succession review - rewards); maintain one HR information system; make the HRD department the integrator and line managers the owners; and audit the linkages every year.

### 6. Conclusion

Poor coordination among HRD subsystems breaks the chain that converts assessment into insight, insight into learning, learning into performance and performance into career growth. Each subsystem may look adequate on its own, yet employee development suffers because the outputs of one never become the inputs of the next. Effectiveness comes from integration, not from the excellence of isolated parts.

## Q34 | 8 | Analyze how HRD practices can be aligned with organizational strategy in a rapidly changing organization.

### 1. The challenge

A rapidly changing organisation - one facing new technology, new markets, mergers, fast growth or regulatory change - has a strategy that itself keeps evolving. Aligning HRD with strategy in such a context is harder than in a stable firm because the competency requirements shift, time to build capability is short, and people are under stress from continuous change. Alignment therefore has to be **dynamic**: not a one-time match but a continuous process of sensing strategic direction, translating it into capability requirements and adjusting HRD practices quickly. The design principles of **strategy alignment, scalability and flexibility, needs-driven approach, technology integration and continuous improvement** are directly relevant.

### 2. Mechanisms of alignment

#### (a) Put HRD at the strategy table

The HRD head should participate in strategic planning so that the people implications of every strategic option are considered and HRD hears about changes early. HRD acts as strategic advisor, not as a downstream service.

#### (b) Translate strategy into a living competency framework

For each strategic thrust, identify the critical roles and the competencies they require. In a changing organisation, review the framework every six to twelve months rather than every five years. Example: a shift to digital sales creates needs for data analysis, online customer engagement and rapid experimentation.

#### (c) Continuous, fast needs assessment

Replace the annual training-needs survey with continuous inputs - performance dashboards, project reviews, pulse surveys and manager conversations - and short diagnostic cycles for each new initiative.

#### (d) Align every subsystem to the strategic priorities

| Subsystem | Alignment action in a changing organisation |
|---|---|
| Performance appraisal | Set shorter goal cycles (quarterly) linked to current strategic priorities; include change-related behaviours (learning agility, collaboration) in KRAs |
| Training and development | Modular, just-in-time learning; heavy use of e-learning and micro-learning; re-skilling programmes tied to strategic projects; action learning on real business problems |
| Potential appraisal | Assess learning agility, adaptability and change leadership, not only current competence |
| Career planning | Flexible, lateral and project-based career paths instead of rigid ladders; emphasise employability and multi-skilling |
| Succession planning | Talent pools for emerging roles, not only existing positions; scenario-based planning |
| Feedback and counselling | Frequent, informal feedback; counselling to manage stress, uncertainty and resistance to change |
| Rewards | Recognise learning, innovation and successful change efforts; team-based rewards for cross-functional projects |
| Organisation development | Change management, communication, team building across new structures; build a culture of experimentation (OCTAPACE) |

#### (e) Build the organisation's capacity to learn

Develop internal trainers, communities of practice and knowledge management so that learning spreads faster than any central training department can deliver. Make line managers coaches; development of people should be part of their goals.

#### (f) Use technology

Learning management systems, mobile learning, virtual classrooms and analytics allow rapid deployment of new content across locations and real-time tracking of skill inventories against strategic needs.

#### (g) Measure alignment and adjust

Track strategic-capability indicators - percentage of critical roles filled with ready people, time to competence in new skills, project outcomes - and review HRD plans quarterly with business leaders. Feed results back into strategy (for example, if capability cannot be built in time, the strategy may need to buy, partner or re-phase).

### 3. Analysis of tensions to be managed

- **Speed versus depth:** rapid modular learning must be balanced with deeper development of leaders.
- **Stability versus flexibility:** people need some predictability of careers even as roles change; transparent communication is essential.
- **Short-term performance versus long-term capability:** pressure to deliver today can crowd out development; protected learning time and leadership commitment are required.
- **Standardisation versus customisation:** common frameworks keep coherence, while local units need freedom to adapt.

### 4. Example

A retail bank moving rapidly to digital channels reviewed its competency framework every six months, created "digital academies" with e-learning modules, ran quarterly goal setting, redefined branch manager careers around customer advisory roles, assessed potential on adaptability, rewarded staff who moved customers to digital services and ran change-management workshops in every region. Within two years most branch staff were competent in the new model and attrition during the transition stayed low.

### 5. Conclusion

In a rapidly changing organisation, HRD is aligned with strategy by sitting at the strategy table, translating strategy into a continuously updated competency framework, sensing needs in real time, tuning every subsystem to current priorities, building the organisation's own learning capacity, using technology and measuring results quarterly. Alignment becomes a continuous loop rather than an annual exercise - which is exactly what the principles of flexibility and continuous improvement demand.

## Q35 | 8 | Evaluate the effectiveness of using potential appraisal together with performance appraisal for employee development.

### 1. The two appraisals

**Performance appraisal** assesses what an employee has actually achieved in the present role during a past period. **Potential appraisal** assesses what the employee is capable of achieving in higher or different roles in the future. Pareek and Rao observed that most Indian organisations collect potential data on the performance appraisal form, which mixes the two; they also warned that past performance is not necessarily an indication of future potential. The question is whether using the two together - as distinct but linked subsystems - is more effective for development than using either alone.

### 2. Arguments supporting the combined use

1. **Complete picture of the employee.** Performance shows current competence and results; potential shows capacity for growth. Together they answer both "How is the person doing?" and "How far can the person go?"
2. **Avoids the promotion trap.** Promoting purely on performance can place an excellent technician in a management role he cannot handle (the Peter Principle). Potential appraisal checks readiness for the new competencies.
3. **Discovers hidden talent.** An average performer in an unsuitable job may have high potential elsewhere; potential appraisal identifies such people whom performance data would overlook.
4. **Targets development precisely.** Performance appraisal identifies present skill gaps; potential appraisal identifies competencies needed for the future role. The individual development plan can then address both.
5. **Enables the nine-box talent grid.** Plotting performance against potential classifies employees - for example high-performance/high-potential (future leaders: accelerate), high-performance/low-potential (valued specialists: retain and reward), low-performance/high-potential (misfit or new: coach and re-place), low/low (counsel and correct) - and prescribes a distinct development strategy for each.
6. **Strengthens career and succession planning.** Career plans based on both are realistic; succession pools contain people who both perform and can grow.
7. **Motivates through fairness.** Employees see that both present contribution and future capability are recognised; high performers who lack leadership potential can be given expert career tracks instead of being frustrated.

### 3. Limitations and risks

| Limitation | Evaluation |
|---|---|
| Potential is harder to measure; assessments may be subjective | Mitigated by defined competencies, multiple methods (assessment centres, tests) and multiple assessors |
| Halo effect - high performers rated high on potential automatically | Requires separate forms, separate timing and trained assessors |
| Labelling - "low potential" ratings can demotivate | Ratings should be developmental, confidential and revisable; potential can grow |
| Cost and time of assessment centres | Focus potential appraisal on critical roles and levels; use simpler methods for others |
| Raised expectations of promotion | Communicate that potential identification leads to development opportunities, not guaranteed promotion |
| Cultural readiness | In low-trust climates, potential ratings may be politicised; build openness first |

### 4. Conditions for effectiveness

The two appraisals should be conceptually and procedurally separate but jointly used; competencies for future roles must be defined; assessors must be trained; results must lead to development plans, career moves and succession decisions; and the process must be transparent and fair.

### 5. Illustration

A pharmaceutical company that introduced a separate potential review found that 20 per cent of its top performers were unsuitable for people-management roles and created a technical specialist ladder for them, while several mid-level performers with high potential were given rotations and later became successful managers. Both groups reported higher satisfaction, and the failure rate of new managers fell.

### 6. Conclusion

On evaluation, using potential appraisal together with performance appraisal is significantly more effective for employee development than using performance appraisal alone. The combination gives a complete picture, prevents wrong promotions, discovers hidden talent, targets development for present and future and supports career and succession planning. Its limitations - subjectivity, halo, cost and labelling - are real but manageable through sound design. The key is to keep the two assessments distinct in method while integrating their results in development planning.

## Q36 | 8 | Analyze how an HRD system can become ineffective when its practices are designed without organizational strategy alignment.

### 1. Introduction

The first principle of HRD system design is **organisational strategy alignment**: HRD should build the talents, knowledge and abilities that the organisation's strategic aims require. When HRD practices are designed in isolation - by copying other companies, by following fashion, by responding to individual requests or simply by repeating last year's plan - the system may look busy and well-organised but fails to produce what the organisation needs. The analysis below shows how misalignment makes each part of the HRD system ineffective and how the effects compound.

### 2. How misalignment makes each practice ineffective

| HRD practice | Effect of designing it without strategy alignment |
|---|---|
| Training needs assessment | Needs are identified from individual wish-lists or generic surveys, not from strategic capability gaps; the organisation trains for yesterday's business |
| Training programmes | Catalogue-driven, generic courses; heavy spending on popular topics while strategic skills (for example digital, quality, new markets) remain undeveloped |
| Performance appraisal | KRAs reflect routine tasks rather than strategic priorities; employees are rewarded for activities that do not move the strategy |
| Potential appraisal | Potential is judged against yesterday's leadership model; the organisation grooms leaders for roles that will not exist and misses the competencies the future requires |
| Career planning | Career paths follow the old structure; when strategy changes the structure, plans become obsolete and employees feel betrayed |
| Succession planning | Successors are prepared for current positions, not for emerging critical roles; strategic initiatives stall for lack of ready leaders |
| Rewards | Reward what is easy to measure rather than what strategy needs; behaviour drifts away from strategic intent |
| Organisation development | Culture-building efforts promote values (for example stability) that contradict the strategy (for example innovation) |

### 3. Systemic consequences

1. **Capability gaps at critical moments.** When the strategy demands new skills - a product launch, a technology shift, an acquisition - the people are not ready, and the strategy is delayed or fails.
2. **Wasted resources.** Training budgets produce programmes, certificates and attendance figures but no business results; return on investment is poor or unmeasurable.
3. **Contradictory signals.** Appraisal, rewards and training push employees in different directions from the strategy communicated by top management; employees become confused and cynical.
4. **Fragmentation of subsystems.** Strategy is the common reference point that integrates the subsystems; without it each subsystem optimises its own activity and coordination collapses.
5. **Loss of credibility and status of HRD.** Business leaders see no link between HRD activity and results, so HRD is treated as a cost centre, excluded from decisions and the first to be cut in a downturn - which is the opposite of the principle of ensuring respectability for the function.
6. **Talent leaves.** High-potential employees notice that development leads nowhere strategically and move to organisations where growth is connected to real opportunities.
7. **Inability to manage change.** Strategic change needs prepared, willing people; a misaligned HRD system has neither prepared them nor built the change-ready culture, so resistance is high.
8. **Measurement failure.** Without strategic goals, HRD cannot define meaningful KPIs; it measures inputs (training days) rather than outcomes, so it cannot learn or improve.

### 4. The vicious circle

```
 Practices designed without strategy --> No visible business impact --> HRD loses credibility
        ^                                                                  and budget
        |                                                                       |
        +------ HRD retreats to safe, generic activities  <----------------------+
```

### 5. Illustration

A manufacturing firm decided to compete on rapid customisation for customers. Its HRD department, working from an old plan, continued to run generic supervisory courses, appraised operators on output volume alone, rewarded long production runs and promoted the most senior foremen. Set-up times did not fall, customer complaints rose, and the strategy was abandoned within two years. The HRD activities were well run - and completely irrelevant.

### 6. Conclusion

An HRD system designed without alignment to organisational strategy becomes ineffective because every practice - needs assessment, training, appraisal, potential appraisal, career and succession planning, rewards and OD - ends up building the wrong capabilities, sending contradictory signals and wasting resources. The system may function smoothly as a set of activities, yet it fails at its real purpose: enabling the organisation to achieve its goals. Alignment is not an optional refinement; it is what gives an HRD system its direction and its value.

## Q37 | 8 | State any two benefits of integrating career planning with potential appraisal.

### 1. Meaning of integration

**Potential appraisal** assesses an employee's capability to perform higher or different roles in the future. **Career planning** maps the path by which the employee will move towards future roles in line with his or her aspirations and the organisation's needs. Integrating the two means that career plans are built on the objective evidence of potential appraisal, and potential appraisal results are always followed by concrete career actions. As the prescribed notes put it, "a meaningful career planning system should be based on potential appraisal." Two major benefits are explained below, followed by others.

### 2. Benefit 1 - Realistic and objective career plans (the right people on the right paths)

- Career planning based only on aspiration or seniority produces unrealistic plans: employees expect roles for which they lack the competencies, and the organisation promotes people who then fail.
- Potential appraisal - through assessment centres, tests and structured evaluation against role requirements - supplies **objective evidence** of strengths and development areas. When career plans are built on this evidence, the destination chosen for each employee matches capability: managerial tracks for those with leadership potential, specialist or expert tracks for strong technical performers with limited managerial potential.
- Development actions on the career path (training, rotation, projects) can be **targeted** to the specific gaps that the potential appraisal revealed, so time and money are not wasted.
- **Result:** fewer wrong promotions, higher success rate in new roles, and career plans that both the employee and the organisation believe in.

### 3. Benefit 2 - Better retention and motivation of talent, and a stronger succession pipeline

- Potential appraisal without career planning is frustrating: employees learn that they have potential but see nothing happen, and the most talented leave. Career planning without potential appraisal is unconvincing: employees suspect that paths are decided by favouritism.
- When the two are integrated, high-potential employees see a **visible, credible future** - a defined path, milestones and development support - which satisfies their growth needs and increases commitment. Career advancement is the prime motivating factor for most managers; integration makes advancement transparent and earned.
- For the organisation, the integrated data produce a **ready talent pool** for succession planning: for each key position, the organisation knows who has the potential, where they are on their career path and when they will be ready. Key positions are filled internally, quickly and with prepared people.
- **Result:** lower attrition of high performers, higher morale, continuity of leadership and reduced recruitment cost.

### 4. Other benefits

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Fairness and equity | Decisions are based on assessed potential rather than impressions, which strengthens the HRD climate |
| Early identification and longer development runway | Potential is spotted early, so the career path has time to build the competencies needed |
| Better use of human resources | People are placed where their capabilities are most valuable |
| Integration of individual and organisational needs | Aspirations (career planning) and capability (potential appraisal) are reconciled with the organisation's manpower plan |
| Improved training effectiveness | Training is planned as steps on a career path rather than as isolated events |
| Reduced conflict and politics | Transparent criteria reduce lobbying for promotions |

### 5. Conclusion

Integrating career planning with potential appraisal yields, first, realistic and objective career plans that place the right people on the right paths, and second, higher motivation and retention of talent together with a dependable succession pipeline. The two subsystems are complementary: potential appraisal tells the organisation what an employee can become, and career planning ensures that he or she actually gets there.

## Q38 | 8 | Mention any two features of an effective HRD practice.

### 1. Meaning of an effective HRD practice

An HRD practice is any planned mechanism - performance appraisal, training, counselling, career planning, potential appraisal, mentoring, rewards, OD interventions - used to develop people. It is **effective** when it actually improves competence, commitment and performance and contributes to organisational goals, rather than merely being conducted. From the principles of HRD system design (strategy alignment, needs-driven approach, integration, line involvement, monitoring, flexibility, technology, measurement) several features of an effective practice can be derived. Two are explained in detail below, followed by a list of others.

### 2. Feature 1 - It is need-based and aligned with organisational goals

- An effective practice starts from a **diagnosed need** - a gap between required and actual performance or competence (Standard performance - Actual performance = Gap) - identified through organisation, task and person analysis, appraisal data or performance analysis.
- The need must in turn be connected to **organisational strategy and goals**, so that the practice builds capabilities the organisation actually requires, now or in the future.
- Because it is need-based and aligned, the practice has clear **objectives** stated in terms of what participants will be able to do and what business result is expected; this also makes evaluation possible.
- Practices that lack this feature - catalogue training, appraisal forms copied from another company, generic "motivation" workshops - consume resources without changing anything. Studies of Indian organisations found training treated as a fringe benefit precisely because needs were not analysed.
- **Example:** A retail chain that finds, through customer-complaint analysis and appraisal data, that billing staff lack knowledge of a new returns policy designs a short, focused programme with the objective "process a return correctly within three minutes," and measures complaint reduction afterwards.

### 3. Feature 2 - It is integrated with other HRD subsystems and supported by line managers (so that learning is applied)

- An effective practice does not stand alone. It **receives inputs** from other subsystems (training needs from appraisal and counselling; career moves from potential appraisal) and **delivers outputs** to them (improved performance to the next appraisal; evidence for career decisions; recognition through rewards).
- It is **owned by line managers**, who set expectations before the practice, coach during it and provide opportunities to apply new skills afterwards. HRD professionals design and facilitate, but action is the responsibility of line people.
- This integration and support ensure **transfer of learning** to the job - the point at which most training fails - and reinforce the behaviour through appraisal and rewards.
- **Example:** After a coaching-skills workshop, managers' appraisals include a KRA on developing subordinates, their bosses review coaching conversations quarterly, and the reward system recognises managers whose team members are promoted.

### 4. Other features of an effective HRD practice

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clear, measurable objectives | States what will change and how it will be measured |
| Participative and developmental | Involves the employee (self-appraisal, joint goal setting, choice of learning); focuses on growth, not control |
| Appropriate method and design | Method fits the objective, the learners and the context (on-the-job, off-the-job, e-learning); follows adult-learning principles |
| Timely and continuous | Delivered when needed, followed up, not a one-off event |
| Top-management commitment | Visibly supported and resourced by leaders |
| Fair, transparent and trusted | Consistent criteria, confidentiality, equity - which sustain the HRD climate |
| Evaluated and improved | Assessed at reaction, learning, behaviour and results levels; revised on the basis of evidence |
| Flexible and scalable | Adapts to changing needs and organisational size |
| Cost-effective and sustainable | Uses internal resources where possible and is budgeted for the long term |

### 5. Conclusion

Two defining features of an effective HRD practice are that it is need-based and aligned with organisational goals, and that it is integrated with the other HRD subsystems and supported by line managers so that learning is applied on the job. When these features are present - along with clear objectives, sound design, fairness and evaluation - an HRD practice moves from being an activity to being a contributor to individual and organisational performance.

## Q39 | 8 | Design an integrated HRD system for an organization that wants to connect performance, potential, training, career planning, feedback, and counselling.

### 1. Design objective and principles

The organisation wants its HRD subsystems to work as one connected system rather than as separate activities. The design therefore follows the principles of **integration and coherence**, **strategy alignment**, **needs-driven approach**, **line-manager involvement**, **monitoring and review** and **phased implementation**. The central design idea is a **common competency framework** and a **single annual HRD calendar** through which the output of each subsystem becomes the input of the next.

### 3. How each subsystem is connected

| Subsystem | Receives from | Gives to | Connecting tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Performance appraisal | Strategy (goals), competency framework | Feedback and counselling, rewards, potential appraisal | KRA sheet linked to unit goals; competency ratings |
| Feedback and counselling | Appraisal data, self-appraisal | IDP, training, career planning | Structured counselling format; IDP template |
| Potential appraisal | Performance history, competency framework for higher roles | Career planning, succession planning, training | Separate potential form; assessment centre report |
| Career planning | Potential results, employee aspirations, manpower plan | IDP, job rotation, succession plan | Career path charts; career counselling record |
| Training and development | IDPs, TNA, career plans | Next appraisal (improved performance), potential evidence | Consolidated training plan; post-training action plan |

| HRIS and reviews | All subsystems | Management and strategy | Dashboard; annual HRD audit |

### 4. Annual integrated calendar

| Period | Activity |
|---|---|
| April | Goal setting for all employees; IDPs from previous cycle activated; training plan launched |
| April - September | Coaching, on-the-job development, training delivery, mentoring |
| October | Mid-year review and feedback; training plan adjusted |
| January - February | Self-appraisal, appraisal review, feedback and counselling, new IDP |
| February | Potential appraisal for identified levels; career counselling; succession review |
| March | Reward decisions; consolidated TNA from IDPs; HRD climate survey; HRD audit and plan for next year |

### 5. Roles, structure and enablers

- **Top management:** approves HRD philosophy, reviews the talent pool and HRD dashboard quarterly.
- **Line managers:** own goal setting, coaching, feedback, counselling and application of learning; "development of subordinates" is one of their KRAs.
- **HRD department (headed by a senior manager):** designs and integrates the system, trains appraisers and counsellors, runs assessment centres, consolidates training and maintains the HRIS.
- **Employees:** participate through self-appraisal, career aspiration statements and ownership of their IDP.
- **Enablers:** trained appraisers and counsellors; one HRIS holding appraisal, potential, career, training and reward data; an OCTAPACE climate of openness and trust; protected budget and learning time.

### 6. Phased implementation

1. **Phase 1 (months 1-6):** competency framework for critical roles; redesign appraisal with goal setting, self-appraisal and counselling; train managers.
2. **Phase 2 (months 6-12):** IDP-based training plan; e-learning platform; mentoring programme.
3. **Phase 3 (year 2):** potential appraisal and career planning for managerial levels; succession plans for key positions; reward linkage.
4. **Phase 4 (year 2 onwards):** extend to all levels and locations (geographical and vertical phasing); annual HRD audit.

### 7. Evaluation of the system

Measures: percentage of employees with completed IDPs; training evaluated at behaviour and results levels; percentage of key positions with ready successors; internal promotion rate; retention of high potentials; HRD climate scores; business KPIs linked to HRD goals.

### 8. Conclusion

The proposed integrated HRD system connects performance, potential, training, career planning, feedback and counselling through a common competency framework, a single annual calendar, the individual development plan as the linking document, a shared information system and clearly assigned roles. Each subsystem feeds the next, so that assessment leads to insight, insight to learning, learning to performance and career growth, and the whole cycle to organisational effectiveness.

## Q40 | 8 | Evaluate a proposed HRD practice that has strong training content but weak linkage with organizational strategy, performance appraisal, and career planning.

### 1. Description of the proposal

The proposed practice is a training intervention - well-designed modules, expert trainers, good materials, sound learning methods - but it has been developed **without reference to organisational strategy**, **without input from or output to performance appraisal**, and **without connection to career planning**. In other words, it is a strong stand-alone product with weak linkages. The evaluation below examines its strengths, its weaknesses, the likely outcomes and the changes needed.

### 2. Strengths of the proposal

1. **Quality of content and delivery** - participants are likely to learn; reaction (Kirkpatrick Level 1) and learning (Level 2) scores will probably be high.
2. **Professional design** - clear modules and good trainers indicate instructional competence in the HRD team.
3. **Immediate morale benefit** - employees usually appreciate high-quality training and see it as a sign that the organisation cares.
4. **Reusable asset** - the content can become valuable once the linkages are built.

### 3. Weaknesses and their consequences

| Missing linkage | Why it matters | Likely consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Organisational strategy | Strategy defines which capabilities matter; without it the training may build irrelevant skills | Content may be excellent but unnecessary; no visible business impact; management questions the spend; strategic skill gaps remain |
| Performance appraisal | Appraisal identifies who needs what (input) and measures whether behaviour changed (output) | Wrong participants; no expectation of new behaviour on the job; no transfer of learning; effectiveness cannot be shown beyond Level 2 |
| Career planning | Career plans give learners a reason to apply and retain learning and tell the organisation how the skills will be used | Learning seen as an event, not a step in growth; no follow-up assignments; skills decay; high potentials still leave |

Additional weaknesses follow from the design principles: it is not **needs-driven** (no diagnostic basis), not **integrated** (no coherence with other subsystems), lacks **line-manager ownership**, and has no basis for **accountability and measurement** at the behaviour and results levels.

### 4. Evaluation against the Kirkpatrick levels

```
 Level 1 Reaction   : likely HIGH   (good content, good trainers)
 Level 2 Learning   : likely HIGH   (sound methods)
 Level 3 Behaviour  : likely LOW    (no appraisal expectation, no supervisor reinforcement, no career use)
 Level 4 Results    : likely NIL / UNMEASURABLE (no strategic objective to measure against)
```

This is the classic pattern of training that people enjoy but that does not change performance - the "fringe benefit" training criticised in studies of Indian organisations.

### 5. Overall judgement

The proposal is **strong as a product but weak as an HRD practice**. Effectiveness in HRD is judged not by the quality of the classroom experience but by improved performance and contribution to organisational goals. On that criterion the proposal, as it stands, is likely to give a poor return on investment and should not be approved in its present form. However, because the content is strong, it should be **modified rather than rejected**.

### 6. Recommendations to strengthen the linkages

1. **Link to strategy:** map each module to a strategic capability requirement; drop or redesign modules that do not serve one; define the business outcome each module is expected to influence.
2. **Link to performance appraisal (input):** select participants on the basis of appraisal-identified needs and IDPs; set pre-training objectives with the supervisor.
3. **Link to performance appraisal (output):** include the trained behaviours in the next appraisal's KRAs or competency ratings; require a post-training action plan reviewed by the manager.
4. **Link to career planning:** position the programme as a step on defined career paths; follow it with job assignments, projects or rotations that use the new skills; record completion in the career and succession database.
5. **Involve line managers:** pre-briefing and post-training review by supervisors; recognise managers who support application.
6. **Evaluate at all four levels:** reaction and learning in class; behaviour through appraisal and 360-degree feedback after three months; results through the business metrics identified in step 1.
7. **Phase and pilot:** run the linked version in one unit, measure, refine and then scale.

### 7. Conclusion

Strong training content is necessary but not sufficient. Without linkage to strategy the training may build the wrong skills; without linkage to appraisal the right people may not attend and new behaviour will not be expected or measured; without linkage to career planning the learning will not be used or retained. The proposal should be approved only after these linkages are built, converting a good training programme into an effective HRD practice.
