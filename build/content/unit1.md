# Unit 1 - HRD Concepts, HRD Climate and the HRD Professional

This unit builds the foundation: what Human Resource Development means, how it grew from simple job training into a strategic function, how it differs from and relates to HRM, what an HRD climate is, and what an HRD professional actually does in an organisation.

> [key] Names to remember for this unit: **Leonard Nadler** (coined the term HRD in 1969), **T. V. Rao and Udai Pareek** (fathers of HRD in India, L&T 1975), **Rao and Abraham** (HRD climate survey and the **OCTAPACE** culture), **M. M. Khan** (definition of HRD).

## Q1 | 8 | Explain the concept of Human Resource Development (HRD) and state its major objectives.

### 1. Meaning of HRD

Human Resource Development (HRD) is the part of human resource management that deals specifically with the **training, learning and development of people** in an organisation. It is a planned and continuous effort to help employees acquire the knowledge, skills, attitudes and capabilities that they need for their present job, their future roles and their overall growth as individuals.

Unlike money, machines or material, human beings have almost **unlimited potential**. HRD is the process of creating an environment in which this potential is continuously identified, brought to the surface, nurtured and used for the benefit of both the individual and the organisation.

### 2. Definitions

- **Leonard Nadler (1969)**, who first used the term HRD at a conference in the USA, defined it as "a series of organised activities, conducted within a specified time and designed to produce behavioural change."
- **Prof. T. V. Rao** defined HRD as "a process by which the employees of an organisation are helped, in a continuous and planned way, to (i) acquire or sharpen capabilities required to perform various functions associated with their present or expected future roles; (ii) develop their general capabilities as individuals and discover and exploit their own inner potential; and (iii) develop an organisational culture in which superior-subordinate relationships, teamwork and collaboration among sub-units are strong."
- **M. M. Khan** described HRD as "the process of increasing knowledge, capabilities and positive work attitudes of all people working at all levels in a business undertaking."

### 3. The concept explained

The concept of HRD rests on three ideas that appear in Rao's definition:

| Dimension | What HRD does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present and future roles | Sharpens the competencies needed for the current job and prepares people for the next role | A sales executive is trained in negotiation now and in team leadership for a future manager role |
| Individual potential | Helps a person discover and use hidden talent, not only job skills | A technician who shows an aptitude for teaching becomes an internal trainer |
| Organisational culture | Builds openness, trust, teamwork and collaboration between departments | Cross-functional projects, open feedback and mentoring |

HRD is therefore not a one-time event. Its key features are that it is **systematic** (planned at individual and organisational level), **continuous** (from joining till retirement), **multi-disciplinary** (draws from psychology, management, economics and sociology), **all-pervasive** (needed in every type of organisation) and works through **specific mechanisms** such as performance appraisal, training, career planning, counselling, potential appraisal and organisation development.

```
              PEOPLE  +  PLANNED LEARNING  +  SUPPORTIVE CLIMATE
                                |
                                v
   Competent individuals --> Effective teams --> High-performing organisation
```

### 4. Major objectives of HRD

1. **Equity** - to treat every employee at par irrespective of caste, creed, religion, gender or language, and to give equal opportunity in training, promotion, career planning and quality of work life.
2. **Employability** - to improve the skills and competencies of employees so that they remain capable of gainful employment anywhere, which in turn motivates them to work effectively.
3. **Adaptability** - to develop, through continuous training, the ability of employees to adjust to technological, structural and market changes.
4. **Competence building** - to develop the technical, managerial, behavioural and conceptual skills required for present and future roles.
5. **Developing a healthy culture** - to create a climate of openness, trust, collaboration and pro-activity (OCTAPACE) in which people enjoy working.
6. **Motivation and commitment** - to satisfy the higher-order needs of employees (recognition, growth, self-actualisation) so that they feel committed to the organisation.
7. **Team spirit and collaboration** - to strengthen superior-subordinate relationships and inter-departmental cooperation.
8. **Organisational effectiveness** - to improve productivity, quality, problem-solving capability and readiness for change, thereby achieving organisational goals.
9. **Future readiness** - to identify and prepare people for succession so that the organisation never faces a leadership vacuum.

### 5. Conclusion

HRD is the planned, continuous and people-centred process of building capability and a development-oriented culture. Its objectives - equity, employability, adaptability, competence, commitment and organisational effectiveness - make it the engine that converts human potential into organisational performance.

## Q2 | 8 | Describe the evolution of Human Resource Development from traditional employee training to a strategic organizational function.

### 1. Introduction

HRD did not appear suddenly. It grew, over about two centuries, from informal skill-passing between a master and an apprentice to a boardroom-level function that shapes business strategy. Each stage of this evolution added a new layer of purpose to the earlier one.

### 2. Stages in the evolution of HRD

#### Stage 1 - Apprenticeship and craft training (before the 18th century)

Skills were transferred informally from a master craftsman to an apprentice in guilds. Learning was slow, individual and limited to manual trades. There was no concept of an organisation "developing" its people.

#### Stage 2 - Vocational and factory training (Industrial Revolution, 1800s)

Factories needed large numbers of semi-skilled workers quickly. Vocational schools and "factory schools" (for example Hoe and Company, 1872) were set up to teach specific machine operations. Training was **task-focused and reactive**, meant only to make workers productive on a machine.

#### Stage 3 - Human relations movement (1920s-1940s)

The Hawthorne studies showed that attitudes, morale and social relationships affect productivity. Training expanded from pure skills to **supervisory and human-relations training**. During World War II the "Training Within Industry" (TWI) programme produced systematic job-instruction methods that are still used today.

#### Stage 4 - Emergence of the training and development profession (1940s-1960s)

Professional bodies like ASTD (1944) were formed. Training departments appeared in large companies, programmed instruction and management development programmes became common, and training began to be evaluated (Kirkpatrick, 1959).

#### Stage 5 - Birth of HRD as a concept (1969 onwards)

**Leonard Nadler** coined the term "Human Resource Development" in 1969, defining it as organised learning experiences designed to bring about behavioural change. HRD widened the scope from "training for the job" to **training, education and development** of the whole person.

#### Stage 6 - HRD in India and the systems view (1975 onwards)

At Larsen & Toubro in 1975, **Udai Pareek and T. V. Rao** designed the first integrated HRD system in India and recommended that HRD be separated from the personnel department. HRD came to mean a set of inter-linked subsystems - performance appraisal, potential appraisal, feedback and counselling, career planning, training, rewards and OD - working on a shared philosophy of trust and openness. Separate HRD departments became common in the 1980s and 1990s.

#### Stage 7 - Strategic HRD (1990s to the present)

With globalisation, technology and knowledge-based competition, people became the main source of competitive advantage. HRD moved to the strategic level: aligning learning with business strategy, building competencies and a learning organisation, managing talent and succession, acting as a change agent, and measuring return on investment. Today e-learning, learning analytics and continuous digital learning have made HRD a real-time, strategic function.

### 3. Summary of the shift

| Aspect | Traditional training | Strategic HRD |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Fix an immediate skill deficiency | Build long-term capability and competitive advantage |
| Scope | Job skills of operatives | Skills, attitudes, careers, culture and organisation |
| Time frame | Short-term, one-off events | Continuous, planned, life-long |
| Responsibility | Training officer | Top management, line managers and HRD professionals |
| Linkage | Isolated from business plans | Derived from and feeding into business strategy |
| Measurement | Number of programmes conducted | Performance improvement, retention, business results |

### 4. Conclusion

HRD has evolved from the narrow, reactive activity of teaching a worker to operate a machine into a proactive, integrated and strategic function that develops individuals, teams and the organisation's culture. The journey - apprenticeship, vocational training, human relations, professional T&D, Nadler's HRD, the Indian systems approach and finally strategic HRD - shows a steady widening of scope from the job to the whole organisation.

## Q3 | 8 | Explain the relationship between Human Resource Development and Human Resource Management.

### 1. Meaning of the two terms

**Human Resource Management (HRM)** is the overall function of procuring, developing, compensating, maintaining and utilising the human resources of an organisation. It covers manpower planning, recruitment, selection, placement, compensation, welfare, industrial relations, discipline and records.

**Human Resource Development (HRD)** is the developmental part of HRM. It is a continuous and planned process that helps employees acquire and sharpen capabilities, discover their potential and build a collaborative organisational culture through mechanisms such as training, performance appraisal, potential appraisal, career planning, counselling and organisation development.

### 2. Nature of the relationship

```
   +--------------------------------------------------------------+
   |            HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT (the whole)             |
   |  Procurement | DEVELOPMENT (HRD) | Compensation | Maintenance |
   +--------------------------------------------------------------+
        HRM supplies people and policies  -->  HRD develops them
        HRD supplies data and competence  -->  HRM uses them
```

1. **Part and whole** - HRD is a sub-system of HRM. HRM is the umbrella; HRD is its development wing. Without HRM there is nobody to develop; without HRD, HRM would only maintain people without improving them.
2. **HRM provides the inputs for HRD** - recruitment and selection bring in the "raw material"; job analysis defines the competencies needed; HR planning shows future skill requirements. HRD uses all of these to plan development.
3. **HRD strengthens HRM decisions** - performance and potential appraisal data generated by HRD are used by HRM for promotion, transfer, compensation and succession decisions.
4. **Shared objective** - both aim at the effective utilisation of people to achieve organisational goals while satisfying employee needs.
5. **Complementary orientation** - HRM keeps the organisation running (maintenance); HRD keeps it growing (development). Together they create a competent and committed workforce.
6. **Common philosophy** - both assume that people are valuable assets and not costs; HRD carries this philosophy further into a culture of trust, openness and collaboration.

### 3. Differences between HRM and HRD

| Basis | HRM | HRD |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Maintenance-oriented | Development-oriented |
| Structure | An independent function | An inter-dependent, inter-related set of sub-systems |
| Aim | Improve efficiency of employees | Develop the employee and the organisation as a whole |
| Responsibility | Personnel / HR department and personnel manager | All managers at all levels, with HRD department as facilitator |
| Motivation | Mainly monetary incentives and rewards | Satisfaction of higher-order needs - growth, recognition, autonomy |
| Nature | Routine, reactive, procedural | Continuous, proactive, process-oriented |
| Time focus | Present requirements | Present and future roles |

### 4. Example

When a company recruits 50 graduate trainees (an HRM activity), HRD designs their induction, technical training, mentoring and career path. Two years later, the potential appraisal conducted by HRD tells HRM which trainees should be promoted and how they should be compensated. The two functions are thus two sides of the same coin.

### 5. Conclusion

HRM and HRD are not rivals; they are inseparable partners. HRM acquires and maintains human resources, HRD develops them, and each feeds the other with people, information and policies. An organisation that wants both stability and growth needs strong HRM supported by an equally strong HRD.

## Q4 | 8 | Discuss the importance of HRD in improving individual and organizational performance.

### 1. Introduction

Organisations today compete on the knowledge, skills and commitment of their people rather than on machines or capital alone. HRD is the process that converts human potential into performance. Its importance can be seen at two levels - the individual employee and the organisation as a whole - and the two are closely connected: competent and motivated individuals produce a high-performing organisation.

### 2. Importance of HRD for individual performance

1. **Competence** - training and development give employees the knowledge, skills and attitudes needed to perform their present job correctly and their future job confidently.
2. **Clarity of role and expectations** - performance appraisal and feedback tell the employee what is expected and how he or she is doing, which removes confusion and reduces errors.
3. **Motivation and commitment** - when the organisation invests in a person's growth, the person feels valued and works with greater involvement.
4. **Career growth and employability** - career planning, potential appraisal and job rotation prepare employees for higher responsibilities and keep their skills marketable.
5. **Problem-solving ability** - HRD develops analytical, creative and decision-making capabilities so that employees solve problems on their own rather than waiting for instructions.
6. **Adaptability and confidence** - continuous learning helps employees accept new technology and change without fear.
7. **Job satisfaction and self-esteem** - counselling, recognition and rewards make employees feel a sense of pride and achievement.

### 3. Importance of HRD for organisational performance

1. **Higher productivity and quality** - competent people produce more with fewer mistakes, less wastage and less supervision.
2. **Culture of trust and openness** - HRD builds an OCTAPACE culture in which communication is open, collaboration is natural and conflicts are confronted constructively.
3. **Team spirit** - inter-departmental cooperation improves, which is essential for project-based and cross-functional work.
4. **Readiness for change** - an organisation whose people are used to continuous learning can adopt new technology, restructure or enter new markets faster than competitors.
5. **Retention and lower cost** - development opportunities reduce turnover and absenteeism, saving recruitment and training costs.
6. **Better HR planning** - HRD generates objective data on skills, performance and potential, which improves manpower planning, succession planning and placement.
7. **Efficiency culture and goal attainment** - resources are used properly and goals are achieved in a better way, leading to greater organisational effectiveness and profitability.
8. **Employer brand and competitive advantage** - organisations known for developing people attract better talent and enjoy a reputation that competitors cannot easily copy.

### 4. The linkage between the two levels

```
 HRD mechanisms          HRD processes / climate           Outcomes
 (appraisal, training,   (openness, trust, teamwork,       Individual: competent,
  counselling, career  -> pro-activity, learning        ->  committed, satisfied
  planning, rewards, OD)  orientation)                     Organisation: productive,
                                                           adaptive, profitable
```

**Example:** Indian IT companies such as Infosys and TCS invest heavily in induction academies, continuous re-skilling and internal certification. The result is visible at both levels - individual employees move quickly from fresher to project leader, and the organisation can redeploy thousands of people to new technologies within months.

### 5. Conclusion

HRD is important because it works on both blades of the scissors at the same time. It makes individuals more competent, confident and committed, and it makes the organisation more productive, adaptive and effective. Any organisation that neglects HRD will soon find that its people - and therefore its performance - have stopped growing.

## Q5 | 8 | Explain the concept of HRD climate and identify the major characteristics of a supportive HRD climate.

### 1. Meaning of HRD climate

The word **climate** refers to the quality of the internal environment of an organisation as perceived by its members - the atmosphere in which people help, judge, reward, constrain and learn about each other. It influences morale and the attitude of individuals towards their work.

**HRD climate** is that part of the organisational climate which relates to the development of people. It is the general environment and set of circumstances within the organisation that **encourage and support human resource development**. According to Rao and Abraham, HRD climate is the perception of employees about the extent to which the organisation, its top management, its line managers and its personnel policies genuinely value and support the development of employees.

The HRD climate is both a **means to an end** (it makes HRD mechanisms work) and an **end in itself** (a good climate is a sign that HRD has succeeded).

### 2. Components of HRD climate

Rao and Abraham measure HRD climate through three components:

| Component | What it covers | Sample indicator |
|---|---|---|
| General climate | Importance given to human resources by top management and line managers | Top management believes people are the most important resource |
| OCTAPACE culture | Openness, Confrontation, Trust, Autonomy, Pro-activity, Authenticity, Collaboration, Experimentation | Employees freely express ideas and disagreements |
| HRD mechanisms | How seriously appraisal, training, feedback, counselling, career planning and rewards are implemented | Training needs are identified and people apply what they learn |

### 3. Characteristics of a supportive HRD climate

1. **Top management commitment** - leaders believe in developing people, spend time and money on it and participate in HRD activities personally.
2. **People treated as the most important resource** - the organisation is genuinely concerned about employee well-being and treats development as an investment, not a cost.
3. **Strong learning culture** - continuous learning, knowledge sharing and skill up-gradation are valued; mistakes are seen as learning opportunities.
4. **Openness and free communication** - employees can express ideas, problems and disagreements without fear; information flows upward as well as downward.
5. **Trust and authenticity** - people keep their word, do not misuse information and superiors are seen as helpful rather than punishing.
6. **Autonomy and pro-activity** - employees have freedom to plan their work, take initiative and anticipate problems.
7. **Collaboration and team spirit** - cross-functional cooperation is encouraged and success is shared.
8. **Experimentation and risk-taking** - trying out new methods is encouraged and failures are not punished.
9. **Effective HRD mechanisms** - appraisal is developmental, feedback is regular, career paths are clear, and training is based on genuine needs.
10. **Recognition and rewards** - good performance and development efforts are noticed, appreciated and rewarded.
11. **Supportive line managers** - supervisors coach, guide and counsel their subordinates and help them apply new learning on the job.
12. **Clear objectives and work-life balance** - employees know what is expected of them and the organisation cares for their well-being.

### 4. Example

In an organisation with a supportive HRD climate, a young engineer who proposes a new process improvement is encouraged to pilot it (experimentation), is given resources (autonomy), receives honest feedback when it partially fails (openness and confrontation), and is still appreciated for the initiative (trust and recognition). In a weak climate the same engineer would be told to "stick to the job."

### 5. Conclusion

HRD climate is the developmental atmosphere of an organisation. A supportive climate - marked by top management commitment, an OCTAPACE culture and well-implemented HRD mechanisms - is the soil in which training, appraisal and career planning can actually bear fruit. Without it, even the best HRD programmes remain paper exercises.

## Q6 | 8 | Differentiate between HRD climate and organizational culture with suitable examples.

### 1. Meaning of the two concepts

**Organisational culture** is the pattern of shared basic assumptions, values, beliefs and norms that a group has developed over time while learning to cope with its external environment and internal integration. It tells members "the way things are done here." Culture is deep-rooted, largely unwritten and passed on to new members through stories, symbols, rituals and role models. Edgar Schein describes it in three layers - artefacts, espoused values and basic underlying assumptions.

**HRD climate** is the perception of employees about the extent to which the organisation's environment supports their learning and development. It is measured through the general climate (importance given to people), the OCTAPACE values and the effectiveness of HRD mechanisms such as appraisal, training, feedback and career planning. Climate is the **surface-level, felt atmosphere**; culture is the **deep-level, shared belief system**.

### 2. Points of difference

| Basis | HRD climate | Organisational culture |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Perception of the developmental atmosphere at a point of time | Shared values, beliefs and assumptions built over years |
| Depth | Surface level - what employees experience and feel | Deep level - why the organisation behaves the way it does |
| Scope | Specific to the development of human resources | Covers every aspect of organisational life - strategy, customers, ethics, dress, decision making |
| Stability | Relatively dynamic; can change within months with new leadership or new HRD practices | Highly stable; changes slowly over years |
| Measurability | Measured through questionnaires (for example the Rao-Abraham HRD Climate Survey, 38 items) | Studied qualitatively through observation, stories, rituals, symbols |
| Origin | Created by top management support, line manager behaviour and HRD mechanisms | Created by founders' philosophy, history, critical incidents and shared learning |
| Unit of analysis | Individual perceptions aggregated | The collective group |
| Level of awareness | Employees are conscious of it and can describe it | Often taken for granted and not consciously noticed |
| Relationship | Is an outcome or reflection of the culture in the area of HRD | Is the foundation from which climate emerges |

### 3. Examples

- **Example of culture:** In a family-managed manufacturing firm the deep assumption is "seniors know best." Decisions flow from the top and loyalty is valued above initiative. This assumption is rarely stated but everyone follows it.
- **Example of climate:** In the same firm, a new HR head introduces monthly feedback meetings, sponsors employees for external courses and rewards suggestions. Within a year employees report that "the company now cares about our growth" - the HRD climate has improved even though the underlying culture of respect for seniority is still present.
- **Example of interaction:** In a start-up whose culture values experimentation, the HRD climate naturally scores high on autonomy and risk-taking. In a bureaucratic public utility whose culture values rule-following, the HRD climate usually scores low on experimentation unless management makes special efforts.

### 4. Relationship between the two

Culture and climate are related as **root and fruit**. Culture provides the values; climate is how those values are experienced in day-to-day HRD practice. A supportive culture makes it easy to build a good HRD climate, and a sustained good HRD climate, over time, can gradually reshape the culture itself. That is why HRD professionals must diagnose culture before designing climate-building interventions.

### 5. Conclusion

HRD climate is the visible, measurable, development-related atmosphere of an organisation, while organisational culture is the invisible, stable system of shared assumptions that underlies all behaviour. Climate can be improved comparatively quickly through HRD mechanisms; culture changes slowly and shapes how those mechanisms are received.

## Q7 | 8 | Explain the role and responsibilities of an HRD professional in an organization.

### 1. Introduction

An HRD professional is the person (or team) who designs, facilitates and manages the learning and development system of an organisation. Earlier this person was simply a "training officer" who arranged classes. Today the HRD professional is expected to be a strategist, consultant, change agent and counsellor. Pareek and Rao, and later Patricia McLagan's "Models for HRD Practice", describe the multiple roles listed below.

### 2. Major roles of an HRD professional

| Role | What the role involves |
|---|---|
| HR strategic advisor | Advises top management on the people implications of business strategy; links HRD plans to organisational goals |
| HRD systems designer | Designs performance appraisal, potential appraisal, career planning, feedback, counselling and reward systems and links them into one integrated system |
| Learning programme specialist / instructional designer | Identifies training needs, sets objectives, chooses methods, prepares content and materials |
| Trainer / facilitator | Delivers programmes, facilitates group learning, workshops and experiential exercises |
| Individual development and career counsellor | Helps employees assess their strengths, set career goals and plan development; provides performance counselling |
| Performance consultant | Diagnoses performance problems, identifies whether the cause is skill, motivation or system, and recommends the right (training or non-training) solution |
| Organisation change agent | Facilitates change management, team building, culture-building and OD interventions |
| Organisation design consultant | Advises on structure, roles, job design and work systems that support learning |
| Researcher and evaluator | Conducts HRD climate surveys, HRD audits, evaluates training (Kirkpatrick levels) and reports return on investment |
| Administrator | Manages budgets, records, training calendars, vendors, learning management systems and compliance |

### 3. Key responsibilities

1. **Developing HRD philosophy and policy** - drafting statements of belief in people and getting top management commitment.
2. **Training needs assessment** at organisation, task and person level, and preparing the annual training plan.
3. **Designing and delivering** induction, technical, behavioural and management development programmes.
4. **Running the appraisal cycle** - ensuring that performance appraisal is used for development, not just for control, and that feedback and counselling follow.
5. **Career and succession planning** - identifying high-potential employees and preparing them for key positions.
6. **Building the HRD climate** - promoting openness, trust, collaboration and experimentation, and coaching line managers to develop their teams.
7. **Facilitating organisation development** - surveys, team building, change initiatives, quality circles and employee participation.
8. **Evaluation and audit** - measuring the effectiveness of every HRD activity and correcting the system.
9. **Keeping knowledge current** - staying updated on e-learning, learning analytics, competency frameworks and legal requirements.

### 4. Competencies required

An HRD professional needs **business competence** (understanding the industry, strategy and finance), **technical competence** (adult learning, instructional design, appraisal methods, evaluation), **interpersonal competence** (communication, coaching, counselling, influencing, empathy) and **intellectual competence** (diagnosis, data analysis, systems thinking, creativity). Personal credibility and integrity are essential because employees share confidential career and performance issues with the HRD professional.

### 5. Working with line managers

The HRD professional does not develop people alone. Line managers are directly responsible for coaching, feedback and on-the-job development of their teams; the HRD professional supports them with systems, tools, expertise and facilitation. HRD is therefore a **shared responsibility** in which the HRD professional acts as designer, catalyst and partner.

### 6. Conclusion

The HRD professional wears many hats - strategist, system designer, trainer, counsellor, change agent, evaluator and administrator. Their central responsibility is to build a competent, committed and continuously learning workforce and a supportive climate, in partnership with top management and line managers, so that both the employees and the organisation grow together.

## Q8 | 8 | Discuss the significance of HRD for achieving organizational effectiveness and long-term employee development.

### 1. Introduction

**Organisational effectiveness** is the degree to which an organisation achieves its goals - profitability, growth, quality, customer satisfaction, innovation and adaptability - while keeping its members satisfied and committed. **Long-term employee development** means the continuous growth of employees over their entire career, not just training for today's job. HRD is significant because it is the only management function whose direct purpose is to build both at the same time.

### 2. The HRD-effectiveness linkage (Rao's model)

```
   HRD MECHANISMS  -->  HRD PROCESSES / CLIMATE  -->  HRD OUTCOMES  -->  ORGANISATIONAL
   Appraisal, feedback,   Role clarity, openness,       More competent,     EFFECTIVENESS
   counselling, training,  trust, teamwork, pro-activity,  committed, satisfied  Higher productivity,
   career planning,        learning orientation,          people; better teams;  quality, growth,
   rewards, OD             risk-taking                    healthy culture         adaptability, profit
```

The model shows that HRD mechanisms first improve the processes and climate of the organisation, these create developmental outcomes in people, and those outcomes are what finally produce organisational effectiveness.

### 3. Significance for organisational effectiveness

1. **Competent workforce** - HRD ensures that every role is filled by a person with the required knowledge and skills, which raises productivity and reduces errors, rework and supervision cost.
2. **Efficiency culture** - resources are used properly and goals are achieved in a better way because people understand what is expected and how their work contributes.
3. **Adaptability to change** - continuous learning makes the organisation capable of adopting new technology, entering new markets and restructuring without major disruption.
4. **Problem-solving and innovation** - HRD develops diagnostic and creative capabilities so that problems are solved at the source and new ideas emerge from every level.
5. **Teamwork and collaboration** - OD interventions and an OCTAPACE culture reduce inter-departmental conflict and make cross-functional projects successful.
6. **Better HR decisions** - objective appraisal and potential data lead to correct placement, promotion and succession, so the right people are in the right jobs.
7. **Retention and cost saving** - development opportunities reduce turnover, absenteeism and the cost of hiring replacements.
8. **Competitive advantage** - skills, culture and knowledge built through HRD cannot be copied quickly by competitors.

### 4. Significance for long-term employee development

1. **Career progression** - career planning and succession planning give employees a visible growth path from entry to retirement.
2. **Continuous skill renewal** - regular training keeps skills current so that employees never become obsolete.
3. **Discovery of potential** - potential appraisal and assessment centres reveal talents that the present job does not use.
4. **Personal growth** - counselling, mentoring and behavioural training develop confidence, emotional maturity and leadership.
5. **Employability and security** - developed employees are valuable inside and outside the organisation, which gives them psychological security.
6. **Motivation through higher-order needs** - growth, recognition and autonomy satisfy needs that salary alone cannot.

### 5. Example

A public sector bank that introduced a structured HRD system - role-based training, mentoring for new officers, annual potential appraisal and a leadership pipeline - found within five years that branch productivity rose, customer complaints fell and most senior positions were filled internally. Employees, on their part, had clear career paths and continuous learning, which improved morale and reduced attrition of young officers.

### 6. Conclusion

HRD is significant because it serves two masters at once. By building competence, a healthy climate and effective HR systems it makes the organisation productive, adaptive and effective; by providing continuous learning, career paths and personal growth it develops employees for the long term. The two reinforce each other - developed employees make the organisation effective, and an effective organisation can invest more in its people.

## Q9 | 8 | A growing organization is facing skill gaps and declining employee performance. Suggest an HRD-oriented approach to address these issues.

### 1. Understanding the situation

A growing organisation typically hires fast, promotes people quickly into supervisory roles, adopts new technology and expands into new work. In this rush, employees are often placed in roles for which they are not fully prepared, supervisors are not trained to coach, and performance standards become unclear. The result is exactly what the case describes - **skill gaps** (the difference between the competencies required and those possessed) and **declining performance**. An HRD-oriented approach treats this as a development problem to be diagnosed and solved systematically, not as a disciplinary problem.

### 2. Recommended HRD-oriented approach

#### Step 1 - Diagnose before prescribing (Training Needs Assessment)

- **Organisation analysis:** review business goals, growth plans and the competencies the new strategy demands.
- **Task analysis:** identify the key tasks and the knowledge, skills and attitudes (KSAs) each role requires.
- **Person analysis:** compare each employee's actual competence with the required standard using appraisal data, tests, observation and interviews. Training need = Standard performance - Actual performance.
- **Performance analysis:** separate causes that training can solve (lack of skill or knowledge) from causes it cannot (poor tools, unclear goals, low motivation, overload). Non-training causes need system corrections.

#### Step 2 - Strengthen the HRD subsystems

| Subsystem | Action for the growing organisation |
|---|---|
| Performance appraisal | Set clear, measurable KRAs for every role; appraise every six months; use results for development plans, not only for increments |
| Feedback and counselling | Train supervisors to give regular, specific and constructive feedback; hold performance counselling sessions for low performers |
| Training | Design role-based programmes - technical training for skill gaps, supervisory training for newly promoted managers; blend on-the-job (coaching, job rotation) with off-the-job (workshops, e-learning) |
| Potential appraisal and career planning | Identify high-potential employees, give them individual development plans and clear career paths so that growth is planned rather than accidental |
| Mentoring and coaching | Pair experienced employees with new ones; make each manager a coach for his or her team |
| Rewards and recognition | Link rewards to performance improvement and to learning achievements (certifications, skills acquired) |
| Organisation development | Team-building, clear communication of goals, participative problem-solving to rebuild morale |

#### Step 3 - Build a supportive HRD climate

Top management must visibly commit time and budget, line managers must own development of their teams, and the OCTAPACE values - openness, trust, autonomy, experimentation - should be encouraged so that employees are not afraid to admit skill gaps and ask for help.

#### Step 4 - Deliver, transfer and evaluate

- Deliver training in short, spaced modules so that work is not disrupted.
- Ensure **transfer of learning**: supervisors assign projects that use the new skill, and employees prepare action plans after each programme.
- Evaluate using the Kirkpatrick model - reaction, learning, behaviour change on the job and business results (error rates, productivity, customer complaints) - and revise the plan every quarter.

### 3. Action plan at a glance

```
 Month 1-2   TNA, performance analysis, competency mapping, quick fixes to systems
 Month 2-4   Appraisal and feedback system redesigned; supervisors trained as coaches
 Month 3-8   Role-based technical and behavioural training; mentoring launched
 Month 6-12  Potential appraisal, career paths, individual development plans, rewards linked
 Ongoing     Kirkpatrick evaluation, HRD climate survey, quarterly review and correction
```

### 4. Expected outcomes

Skill gaps close because training is targeted; performance rises because expectations are clear and feedback is regular; newly promoted managers become effective coaches; high-potential employees stay because they see a future; and the organisation gains a development culture that supports further growth.

### 5. Conclusion

The HRD-oriented approach begins with diagnosis, corrects the systems that caused the decline, builds competence through integrated HRD subsystems, and sustains the gains through a supportive climate and continuous evaluation. This turns a crisis of skill gaps into an opportunity to institutionalise development in the growing organisation.

## Q10 | 8 | Apply the concept of HRD climate to suggest measures for creating a learning-oriented work environment.

### 1. The concept applied

HRD climate is the perception of employees that the organisation genuinely supports their development. It has three components - the **general climate** (importance given to people by top management and line managers), the **OCTAPACE culture** (Openness, Confrontation, Trust, Autonomy, Pro-activity, Authenticity, Collaboration, Experimentation) and the **HRD mechanisms** (appraisal, feedback, training, career planning, rewards). A learning-oriented work environment is simply an organisation in which all three components score high. The measures below are therefore organised under these three heads.

### 2. Measures to strengthen the general climate

1. **Visible top management commitment** - leaders should state a development philosophy, allocate a fixed percentage of payroll to learning, attend programmes and personally mentor young managers.
2. **Make line managers accountable for development** - include "development of subordinates" as a key result area in every manager's appraisal and reward managers whose team members grow.
3. **Treat people as the most important resource** - communicate business plans and their people implications openly so that employees see why learning matters.
4. **Provide time and resources for learning** - protected learning hours, a library or digital learning platform, and budgets for external courses and certifications.

### 3. Measures to build an OCTAPACE culture

| Value | Practical measure |
|---|---|
| Openness | Open-door practice, regular town-hall meetings, suggestion schemes, upward feedback |
| Confrontation | Train people to discuss problems and disagreements face-to-face with facts, not by avoiding or complaining |
| Trust | Keep commitments, maintain confidentiality of counselling, avoid blame for honest mistakes |
| Autonomy | Delegate authority, allow employees to plan their own work and learning |
| Pro-activity | Encourage anticipation of problems, reward initiative, use "what if" planning |
| Authenticity | Leaders behave consistently with stated values; feedback is honest |
| Collaboration | Cross-functional teams, knowledge-sharing sessions, communities of practice |
| Experimentation | Pilot projects, innovation days, tolerance for failure, "lessons learnt" reviews |

### 4. Measures to strengthen HRD mechanisms

1. **Developmental performance appraisal** - self-appraisal, joint goal setting and a separate development review that is not linked to salary discussion.
2. **Regular feedback and counselling** - quarterly one-to-one conversations; managers trained in coaching and performance counselling.
3. **Need-based training** - a proper training needs assessment every year; programmes followed by action plans and on-the-job application.
4. **Career planning and potential appraisal** - transparent career paths, individual development plans and assessment centres for high-potential employees.
5. **Job rotation and stretch assignments** - learning by doing in new roles, projects and task forces.
6. **Knowledge management** - document lessons from projects, create expert directories, encourage internal trainers and peer teaching.
7. **Reward learning** - recognise certifications, skill acquisition, knowledge sharing and innovation, not only output.
8. **Measure the climate** - conduct an HRD climate survey (for example the 38-item Rao-Abraham instrument) every year, share the results and act on them.

### 5. Example

A mid-sized software firm introduced "Learning Fridays" (two protected hours per week), made every project manager a coach with a target of two developmental conversations per month, launched an internal certification programme linked to rewards, and published the results of its annual HRD climate survey. Within eighteen months, its scores on openness, experimentation and training effectiveness rose sharply, attrition fell and internal promotions increased.

### 6. Conclusion

A learning-oriented environment is created not by one training programme but by raising the whole HRD climate - committed leadership and line managers, OCTAPACE values practised daily, and HRD mechanisms that are genuinely developmental. When all three move together, learning becomes a habit of the organisation rather than an occasional event.

## Q11 | 8 | State any two objectives of Human Resource Development.

### 1. Meaning of HRD objectives

The objectives of HRD are the results that the organisation wants to achieve by investing in the planned and continuous development of its people. The prime objective is to create an organisational environment in which **people come first**. From this prime objective flow several specific objectives, of which the three most frequently cited are **equity, employability and adaptability**. Any two of these can be stated, but the complete list is given below so that the answer is not incomplete.

### 2. Two major objectives explained in detail

#### Objective 1 - Equity

- **Meaning:** Recognising every employee at par, irrespective of caste, creed, religion, gender, region or language.
- **What HRD does:** ensures that the organisation provides equal opportunity to all employees in career planning, promotion, training and development, and quality of work life. Selection for training is based on genuine need, not favouritism; appraisal is objective; career paths are transparent.
- **Why it matters:** Equity builds trust. When employees believe the system is fair, they participate willingly in development activities, accept feedback and stay committed. Inequity, on the other hand, breeds resentment, politics and turnover.
- **Example:** A company that publishes clear eligibility criteria for its management development programme and selects participants through an assessment centre is practising equity.

- **Meaning:** Employability is the ability, skills and competencies of an individual to seek and hold gainful employment anywhere - inside or outside the present organisation.
- **What HRD does:** continuously improves the skills of employees through training, multi-skilling, job rotation and certification so that they remain valuable in the labour market.
- **Why it matters:** In a fast-changing economy, jobs and technologies become obsolete quickly. An employee whose skills are kept current feels secure and motivated to work with effectiveness, while the organisation gains a flexible, redeployable workforce.
- **Example:** An IT firm that re-skills its mainframe programmers in cloud technologies improves both their employability and its own capability.

### 3. Other objectives of HRD (for completeness)

| Objective | Brief meaning |
|---|---|
| Adaptability | Continuous training that helps employees adjust to technological and organisational change |
| Competence development | Building technical, managerial, behavioural and conceptual capabilities for present and future roles |
| Healthy culture | Creating openness, trust, collaboration and pro-activity (OCTAPACE) |
| Motivation and commitment | Satisfying higher-order needs such as recognition, growth and autonomy |
| Team spirit | Strengthening superior-subordinate and inter-departmental relationships |
| Organisational effectiveness | Improving productivity, quality, problem-solving and goal achievement |
| Succession readiness | Preparing people in advance for key future positions |

### 4. Conclusion

Equity and employability are two central objectives of HRD: the first ensures that development opportunities are distributed fairly, and the second ensures that development keeps every employee capable and secure. Together with adaptability, competence building and culture development, they express the basic HRD belief that when people grow, the organisation grows.

## Q12 | 8 | State any two ways in which HRD and HRM are related.

### 1. Introduction

Human Resource Management (HRM) is the total function of procuring, developing, compensating, maintaining and utilising people, whereas Human Resource Development (HRD) is its developmental sub-system - the planned, continuous process of building employee capability through training, appraisal, career planning, counselling and organisation development. Because HRD sits inside HRM, the two are related in several ways. Two important relationships are explained below, followed by a summary of the others.

### 2. Relationship 1 - HRD is an integral sub-system (part) of HRM

- HRM is the whole; HRD is a part. The "development" function of HRM - training, management development, career development and OD - is exactly what HRD covers.
- HRM sets the policy framework (HR philosophy, manpower plan, job descriptions, compensation structure) within which HRD operates. For instance, the manpower plan tells HRD how many people will need what competencies in three years, and job analysis tells HRD what a role requires.
- Without HRM there would be nobody to develop and no framework for development; without HRD, HRM would merely maintain people without improving them.

```
   HRM  =  Procurement + DEVELOPMENT (HRD) + Compensation + Maintenance + Utilisation
```

### 3. Relationship 2 - The two functions exchange inputs and outputs (they are complementary)

- **HRM supplies HRD with people and information.** Recruitment and selection bring in employees who become the "raw material" for development. Induction hands them over to HRD. HR records supply data on qualifications, experience and performance history.
- **HRD supplies HRM with competence and decision data.** Performance appraisal, potential appraisal and assessment centre results produced by HRD are used by HRM for promotion, transfer, placement, succession and compensation decisions. Trained employees produced by HRD are the "output" that HRM deploys and rewards.
- The relationship is therefore a two-way flow: HRM acquires and maintains, HRD develops, and each depends on the quality of the other's work.

**Example:** When a bank recruits probationary officers (HRM), HRD designs their induction, credit training and mentoring. Two years later HRD's potential appraisal tells HRM which officers should be fast-tracked and how they should be rewarded.

### 4. Other ways in which HRD and HRM are related

| Relationship | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Common goal | Both aim at effective use of human resources to achieve organisational goals while satisfying employee needs |
| Common philosophy | Both regard people as valuable assets; HRD extends the philosophy into a culture of trust and openness |
| Complementary orientation | HRM is maintenance-oriented (keeps the organisation running); HRD is development-oriented (keeps it growing) |
| Shared responsibility | HRM is mainly the HR department's job; HRD is every manager's job - but both work through the same line managers |
| Strategic linkage | Strategic HRM cannot succeed without HRD delivering the competencies the strategy requires |

### 5. Conclusion

HRD is related to HRM first as a part is related to the whole, and second as a partner that receives people and policies from HRM and gives back competence and decision data. The two are interdependent: strong HRM without HRD produces a stable but stagnant organisation, while HRD without HRM has nothing to build on.

## Q13 | 8 | Analyze how a weak HRD climate can affect employee behaviour and organizational performance.

### 1. What a weak HRD climate means

HRD climate is the perception of employees that the organisation supports their development. It is weak when top management shows little real interest in people, line managers do not coach or give feedback, the OCTAPACE values (openness, confrontation, trust, autonomy, pro-activity, authenticity, collaboration, experimentation) are absent, and HRD mechanisms such as appraisal, training and career planning exist only on paper. The analysis below traces how such a climate shapes individual behaviour and then how that behaviour damages organisational performance.

### 2. Effects on employee behaviour

1. **Low motivation and involvement** - when development is not valued, employees do only what is required to avoid trouble. Discretionary effort disappears.
2. **Defensive and closed communication** - lack of openness and trust makes people hide problems, avoid honest feedback and communicate only through formal channels. Mistakes are concealed rather than corrected.
3. **Fear of experimentation** - if failures are punished, employees stop taking initiative and stick rigidly to instructions. Innovation dries up.
4. **Dependence and lack of autonomy** - without delegation and pro-activity, employees wait for orders and push every decision upward.
5. **Poor teamwork and politics** - low collaboration and authenticity lead to inter-departmental rivalry, blame games and informal power groups.
6. **Cynicism towards HRD activities** - appraisal is seen as a ritual for increments, training as a holiday, and counselling as a warning session. Employees do not apply what they learn.
7. **Skill stagnation** - without need-based training and job rotation, competencies become obsolete and employees lose confidence.
8. **Withdrawal behaviour** - absenteeism, late-coming, lack of punctuality and, finally, resignation of the best performers who find better development elsewhere.

### 3. Effects on organisational performance

| Behavioural symptom | Organisational consequence |
|---|---|
| Low motivation and skill stagnation | Falling productivity, poor quality, higher rework and customer complaints |
| Concealment of problems | Small issues grow into crises; decisions are taken on wrong information |
| No experimentation | Loss of innovation and competitiveness; slow adoption of technology |
| Dependence on superiors | Slow decision making, overloaded managers, poor responsiveness to customers |
| Politics and poor teamwork | Delays in cross-functional projects, duplication of effort, high conflict cost |
| Cynicism about HRD | Training budgets wasted; appraisal data unreliable, leading to wrong promotions |
| Exit of talent | High recruitment cost, loss of knowledge, weak succession pipeline |

### 4. The chain reaction

```
 Weak HRD climate --> Fear, distrust, low motivation --> Poor performance and no learning
        ^                                                          |
        |________ Management blames employees, tightens control ___|
```

A weak climate creates a vicious circle. Poor performance leads management to tighten control instead of building capability, which further weakens the climate. Talented people leave first, so the average competence level keeps falling.

### 5. Example

A manufacturing unit where the appraisal was used only for confirming increments, feedback was given only when something went wrong and no training needs were ever assessed found that its rejection rate climbed steadily, its young engineers left within two years, and supervisors refused to try a new quality system because "nobody wants to be blamed if it fails."

### 6. Conclusion

A weak HRD climate first changes behaviour - employees become passive, defensive, un-cooperative and disengaged - and this behaviour then shows up in the organisation's results as low productivity, poor quality, weak innovation, high attrition and slow decision making. Strengthening the climate is therefore not a soft, optional activity but a direct lever on organisational performance.

## Q14 | 8 | Analyze the changing role of an HRD professional as organizations move from traditional personnel administration toward strategic human resource development.

### 1. Introduction

The role of the HRD professional has changed along with the way organisations think about people. In the era of **personnel administration** (roughly up to the 1970s), people were a cost to be controlled and the personnel officer's job was to keep records, ensure discipline and arrange occasional training. In the era of **strategic HRD**, people are the main source of competitive advantage, and the HRD professional is expected to help shape and execute business strategy through capability building. The analysis below compares the two roles and explains why the shift happened.

### 2. The traditional personnel-administration role

- **Focus on maintenance:** attendance, leave, payroll, welfare, statutory compliance, discipline and grievances.
- **Training as an event:** a training officer organised programmes from a standard calendar; needs were rarely analysed and results were rarely evaluated.
- **Reactive and rule-bound:** the personnel department responded to problems after they arose and enforced rules made by others.
- **Low status:** the function reported at a middle level and was not consulted on business decisions.
- **Control orientation:** appraisal was used for increments and control, not development; counselling meant warnings.

### 3. The strategic HRD role

| Dimension | Traditional personnel administrator | Strategic HRD professional |
|---|---|---|
| View of people | A cost to be controlled | An asset and a source of competitive advantage |
| Main task | Record keeping, compliance, discipline | Building competencies, culture and leadership pipeline |
| Position | Middle-level staff function | Senior level; partner of the CEO and business heads |
| Time horizon | Present; short term | Present and future; long term |
| Approach | Reactive, procedural | Proactive, diagnostic, consultative |
| Training | Ad hoc programmes, activity counted | Need-based learning aligned with strategy, results measured |
| Appraisal | For control and increments | For development, potential identification and career planning |
| Relationship with line managers | Enforcer of rules | Coach, consultant and partner who makes line managers developers of people |
| Measurement | Number of programmes, training days | Behaviour change, performance improvement, retention, ROI, HRD audit |
| Technology | Manual files | HRIS, e-learning, learning analytics |

### 4. New roles that the HRD professional now performs

1. **Strategic advisor** - translates business strategy into competency requirements and learning strategy.
2. **HRD systems designer** - integrates appraisal, potential appraisal, career planning, feedback, training and rewards into one system.
3. **Performance consultant** - diagnoses performance gaps and recommends training or non-training solutions.
4. **Change agent and OD facilitator** - leads culture change, restructuring and team building.
5. **Talent and succession manager** - identifies high-potential employees and builds a leadership pipeline.
6. **Learning architect** - blends on-the-job, classroom and digital learning; creates a learning organisation.
7. **Evaluator and researcher** - measures HRD climate, evaluates programmes and demonstrates business impact.
8. **Employee advocate and counsellor** - balances organisational needs with employee well-being and career aspirations.

### 5. Why the role changed

- **Competition and globalisation** made speed, quality and innovation - all people-dependent - the basis of survival.
- **Technology** made skills obsolete faster and demanded continuous learning.
- **Knowledge work** shifted value from machines to people's ideas.
- **Research evidence** (Hawthorne studies onwards, and the HRD models of Nadler, Pareek and Rao) showed that development-oriented practices improve performance.
- **Rising employee expectations** for growth, autonomy and meaningful work.

### 6. Challenges in the new role

The strategic HRD professional must earn credibility with business leaders, learn the language of finance and strategy, manage the tension between short-term results and long-term development, and avoid being pulled back into administrative fire-fighting. Competence in business, technology, data analysis and consulting is now as important as knowledge of training methods.

### 7. Conclusion

The HRD professional has moved from being a record-keeper and organiser of classes to being a strategic partner who designs systems, builds culture, develops leaders and measures impact. The shift reflects the movement of organisations themselves - from controlling people as a cost to developing people as their most valuable and least imitable resource.

## Q15 | 8 | Analyze the relationship among HRD philosophy, HRD climate, and organizational performance.

### 1. The three concepts

- **HRD philosophy** is the set of basic beliefs and values that top management holds about people - for example, that people are the most important resource, that every person has unlimited potential, that people can be trusted, that development is a shared responsibility and that a healthy culture is worth investing in. The philosophy is the "why" of HRD.
- **HRD climate** is how those beliefs are actually experienced by employees in daily life - the perceived support for development, measured through the general climate, the OCTAPACE values and the working of HRD mechanisms. The climate is the "felt reality" of HRD.
- **Organisational performance** is the achievement of goals - productivity, quality, profitability, growth, innovation, customer satisfaction and employee retention. It is the "result."

### 2. How the three are related

```
 HRD PHILOSOPHY  -->  HRD PRACTICES  -->  HRD CLIMATE  -->  EMPLOYEE OUTCOMES  -->  ORGANISATIONAL
 (beliefs about       (appraisal,          (openness,        (competence,             PERFORMANCE
  people held by      training, career     trust, autonomy,  commitment,              (productivity,
  top management)     planning, rewards,   collaboration,    satisfaction,            quality, profit,
                      counselling, OD)     experimentation)  teamwork, learning)       growth, retention)
        ^                                                                                    |
        |__________________ feedback: results reinforce or weaken the philosophy _____________|
```

#### Relationship 1 - Philosophy shapes climate

The philosophy of top management determines which HRD practices are introduced and, more importantly, how sincerely they are implemented. If leaders genuinely believe that people can be trusted and developed, they delegate, share information, invest in training and treat appraisal as a development tool. Employees perceive this sincerity, and the HRD climate becomes strong. If the philosophy is control-oriented, the same practices are implemented mechanically and the climate remains weak, however impressive the HR manual looks. In short, **climate is philosophy in action**.

#### Relationship 2 - Climate drives performance

A strong climate produces developmental outcomes - competent, committed, satisfied employees, clear roles, effective teams and a habit of learning. These outcomes translate into higher productivity, better quality, faster problem-solving, innovation, lower attrition and hence better organisational performance. Research by Rao and Abraham and later studies in Indian organisations show a positive relationship between HRD climate scores and measures of performance and commitment.

#### Relationship 3 - Performance feeds back into philosophy

When development-oriented practices visibly produce results, top management's belief in HRD is reinforced and further investment follows - a virtuous circle. When performance is poor, management may either deepen the development effort (if the philosophy is strong) or abandon it and return to control (if the philosophy was shallow).

### 3. Analysis of possible combinations

| Philosophy | Climate | Likely performance |
|---|---|---|
| Strong, development-oriented and practised | Strong | High and sustainable; organisation adapts and grows |
| Development-oriented on paper but not practised | Weak or cynical | Short-term results may be acceptable, but attrition and stagnation follow |
| Control-oriented | Weak | Compliance without commitment; low innovation; talent leaves |
| Changing towards development (new leadership) | Improving | Performance improves gradually as trust is rebuilt |

### 4. Example

Two similar engineering firms adopted the same appraisal format. In Firm A the managing director personally reviewed development plans, managers were rewarded for growing their people and training followed the appraisal - the climate scored high and productivity and retention improved. In Firm B the format was filled in once a year for increments only - employees called it a "formality," the climate remained weak, and the best engineers left. Same practice, different philosophy, different climate, different performance.

### 5. Conclusion

HRD philosophy, HRD climate and organisational performance form a causal chain with a feedback loop. Philosophy is the root, climate is the visible plant and performance is the fruit. An organisation cannot buy a good climate by copying practices; it has to hold and practise the underlying belief in people. When it does, the climate improves and performance follows, which in turn strengthens the philosophy.

## Q16 | 8 | Evaluate whether HRD should be treated as a strategic function rather than only as an employee training activity.

### 1. The issue

Many organisations still equate HRD with "training" - a calendar of programmes run by a training department. The question is whether this narrow view is adequate, or whether HRD deserves to be treated as a **strategic function** - one that is derived from business strategy, integrated with all people systems and represented at the top-management level. The evaluation below considers both sides and reaches a judgement.

### 2. Arguments for treating HRD as a strategic function

1. **People are the source of competitive advantage.** Capital and technology can be bought; a competent, committed and learning workforce cannot. Building such a workforce is a strategic task, not a clerical one.
2. **Strategy needs competencies.** Every strategy - diversification, digitalisation, quality leadership, global expansion - requires specific skills and behaviours. Only a strategic HRD function can anticipate and build them in time.
3. **HRD is a system, not an event.** Training alone cannot improve performance if appraisal, feedback, career planning, rewards and culture pull in other directions. Integration of these subsystems requires strategic authority.
4. **Culture and change.** Organisational change, mergers and new technology succeed or fail on people's readiness. HRD as change agent and culture builder must sit at the strategy table.
5. **Long-term leadership pipeline.** Succession planning and leadership development take years; they must be linked to the long-term plan of the business.
6. **Return on investment.** When HRD is aligned with strategy, training investment produces measurable business results; unaligned training is often wasted (D'Souza's finding that training is treated as a "fringe benefit").
7. **Evidence.** Studies of high-performance organisations - from Indian IT firms to global manufacturers - show that strategic integration of HRD correlates with productivity, innovation and retention.

### 3. Arguments for a narrower, training-only view (and their limitations)

| Argument | Evaluation |
|---|---|
| Training is concrete and easy to manage and budget | True, but managing activity is not the same as producing results; number of training days says nothing about performance |
| Small organisations cannot afford elaborate HRD systems | Strategic HRD is about alignment and integration, not size; even a small firm can link learning to its goals |
| Line managers, not HRD, are responsible for performance | Correct - which is exactly why HRD must strategically equip line managers as developers of people rather than merely run classes |
| Strategy is the domain of top management | People implications are part of every strategic decision; excluding HRD produces plans that cannot be implemented |

### 4. Risks of confining HRD to training

- Training becomes an isolated, reactive activity chosen from vendors' catalogues.
- No linkage with appraisal or career planning, so learning is not applied and motivation stays low.
- HRD loses credibility and budget in downturns because its value cannot be shown.
- The organisation faces skill shortages and leadership gaps at the moment of strategic change.

### 5. Balanced view

Treating HRD as strategic does not mean neglecting training. Training remains the most visible and widely used HRD mechanism. The point is that training should be **one instrument within an integrated, strategy-driven HRD system**, with the HRD head participating in strategic planning, HRD goals derived from business goals, all subsystems linked, line managers accountable for development, and outcomes measured in business terms.

### 6. Conclusion

On evaluation, the case for HRD as a strategic function is stronger. Training alone addresses today's skill deficiencies; strategic HRD builds the competencies, culture and leadership that tomorrow's strategy will need. Organisations that keep HRD at the level of a training activity get programmes; organisations that treat it strategically get performance and sustainable competitive advantage.

| Objective evidence | Low attrition of good performers, high internal promotion, active suggestion schemes |

### 5. Conclusion

Two clear indicators of a supportive HRD climate are (1) visible top-management belief in and commitment to developing people and (2) openness and trust reflected in free, constructive feedback. ## Q18 | 8 | State any two reasons why an HRD professional needs to understand organizational culture.

### 1. Introduction

**Organisational culture** is the pattern of shared assumptions, values and norms that a group has learnt over time - "the way things are done here." It is deep, stable and largely unwritten. An HRD professional works on people's capabilities, behaviour and the developmental climate; all of these are shaped by culture. Understanding culture is therefore not optional background knowledge for the HRD professional but a precondition for effective work. Two principal reasons are analysed below, followed by other supporting reasons.

### 2. Reason 1 - HRD systems must be designed to fit (and gradually shape) the culture

- One of the principles of designing an HRD system is that **HRD systems must be designed differently for different organisations**; the basic principles may be common, but the components, their phasing and their processes must suit the organisation's culture, tradition, size, technology and level of skills.
- A practice that works in one culture may fail in another. Example: an open, multi-rater 360-degree feedback system suits a culture that values openness and confrontation; imposed on a hierarchical culture where subordinates fear seniors, it produces dishonest ratings and resentment. Self-appraisal fails where employees believe that admitting weakness will be punished.
- At the same time, HRD's role is not only to adapt to culture but to **balance adaptation and change** - to move the culture towards openness, trust and collaboration (OCTAPACE). To do this the HRD professional must first know accurately what the present culture is, where it supports development and where it blocks it.
- Understanding culture therefore allows the HRD professional to sequence interventions realistically - building trust before introducing peer feedback, for instance - and to avoid mechanical copying of "best practices."

### 3. Reason 2 - Culture determines whether learning is accepted, transferred and sustained

- Employees interpret every HRD activity through cultural lenses. In a culture where training is seen as a reward or a holiday, participants will not take programmes seriously; in a culture where mistakes are punished, people will not experiment with new skills on the job.
- **Transfer of training** depends on supervisors, peers and norms. If the cultural norm is "do it the way we have always done it," newly trained employees quietly revert to old methods. Rao and Abraham's studies found that employees in many Indian organisations reported little opportunity to try out what they had learnt - a cultural, not a technical, problem.
- Culture also decides which **learning methods** work. Highly collectivist cultures respond to group-based, mentoring and team learning; cultures that value individual achievement respond to competitive assessments and self-paced e-learning.
- An HRD professional who understands these cultural signals can design programmes that are accepted, involve line managers in reinforcing new behaviour and create the conditions in which learning actually changes performance.

### 4. Other reasons

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Diagnosing performance problems correctly | Many "training problems" are really cultural problems (fear, politics, unclear norms); culture knowledge prevents wrong prescriptions |
| Acting as a change agent | Change management and OD are impossible without understanding the values and resistance points of the culture |
| Building HRD climate | Climate emerges from culture; improving it requires working with cultural roots |
| Credibility with line managers and employees | Proposals that respect cultural realities are accepted; culturally blind proposals are resisted |
| Managing sub-cultures and diversity | Different departments, regions and generations may have different cultures needing different approaches |
| Linking HRD to strategy | Strategy execution depends on culture; the HRD professional must align culture, capability and strategy |

### 5. Conclusion

An HRD professional needs to understand organisational culture, first, because HRD systems must be designed to fit the culture and to gradually move it in a developmental direction, and second, because culture decides whether learning is accepted, applied on the job and sustained. Culture is the soil in which HRD practices are planted; without knowing the soil, even the best seeds fail to grow.

## Q19 | 8 | Evaluate the likely consequences of implementing HRD practices without considering the existing organizational culture and climate.

### 1. The issue

Organisations often import HRD practices - 360-degree feedback, assessment centres, forced-ranking appraisal, self-managed teams, e-learning platforms - because they are fashionable or worked elsewhere. The principle of **attention to contextual factors** in HRD system design warns that what is included in the system and how it is introduced must depend on the organisation's culture, tradition, size, technology and existing skills. Ignoring the culture (deep shared assumptions) and the climate (present perceptions about support for development) leads to predictable consequences, evaluated below.

### 2. Likely consequences

#### (a) Resistance and rejection

Practices that clash with cultural assumptions are resisted openly or passively. Example: a hierarchical, seniority-respecting culture asked to give upward feedback to bosses will either refuse or give meaningless positive ratings. The practice survives on paper but dies in practice.

#### (b) Ritualisation - form without substance

When a practice is imposed on an unreceptive climate, people comply mechanically. Appraisal forms are filled at the last minute, training attendance is marked but nothing is learnt, and development plans are copied from previous years. HRD becomes a bureaucratic ritual, which further deepens cynicism.

#### (c) Loss of trust and damage to the existing climate

Half-hearted or mishandled practices can make the climate worse than before. A "developmental" appraisal that is suddenly used to justify lay-offs destroys trust for years. Confidential counselling data leaking into promotion decisions teaches employees never to be open again.

#### (d) Distorted data and wrong decisions

Potential appraisal, ratings and surveys collected in a low-trust climate are inaccurate. Decisions on promotion, succession and training built on this data are wrong, which affects performance and fairness.

#### (e) Failure of transfer of learning

Even good training fails when the culture on the job does not support new behaviour. Employees return from a programme on participative management to a boss who tolerates no questions; the learning is abandoned within weeks.

#### (f) Wasted resources and loss of credibility of HRD

Budgets, time and consultant fees produce no visible result. Top management concludes that "HRD does not work," and future investment is cut. The HRD function itself loses respectability, which is one of the key principles it must protect.

#### (g) Conflict, stress and turnover

Sudden cultural mismatch - for example, forced ranking in a collaborative culture - breeds competition, politics and stress; good performers who value the old culture leave.

#### (h) Sub-culture clashes

Multi-location or merged organisations may have different sub-cultures; a uniform practice may succeed in one plant and fail in another, creating perceptions of inequity.

### 3. Evaluation - is the outcome always negative?

| Situation | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Practice contradicts core cultural assumptions and is imposed without preparation | Rejection, ritualisation, damage to trust - clearly negative |
| Practice is culturally neutral (for example, a better training-needs form) | Minor problems; may succeed with limited adaptation |
| Practice deliberately challenges the culture but is introduced with top-management commitment, communication, pilot testing and support | Can succeed and even shift the culture - HRD's legitimate role is to balance adaptation and change |

So the consequences depend on **how far the practice departs from the culture and how it is introduced**. Ignoring culture is the problem; challenging it thoughtfully is not.

### 4. How to avoid the negative consequences

1. Diagnose culture and measure HRD climate (for example with the Rao-Abraham survey) before designing.
2. Adapt the practice - modify its form, sequence and pace to fit the context.
3. Prepare the ground - build trust and openness, train line managers, communicate the purpose.
4. Pilot in a receptive unit, learn, then scale.
5. Secure visible top-management commitment and align rewards with the new behaviour.
6. Evaluate and revise continuously.

### 5. Conclusion

Implementing HRD practices without regard to culture and climate usually produces resistance, empty rituals, distorted data, wasted resources, loss of trust and eventually loss of credibility for HRD itself. The lesson is not that culture should never be challenged - HRD exists partly to move culture towards openness and development - but that every practice must be introduced with an accurate understanding of the cultural ground and the climate on which it will land.

## Q20 | 8 | Design a basic HRD framework for an organization that wants to improve employee capability while aligning development efforts with organizational goals.

### 1. Design principles behind the framework

A good HRD framework should (i) be **aligned with organisational strategy**, (ii) be **needs-driven**, (iii) be **integrated** so that its subsystems reinforce one another, (iv) **involve line managers** as developers of people, (v) be **scalable and flexible**, (vi) build **enabling capabilities** - not only skills but also problem-solving ability and a healthy culture - and (vii) be **evaluated** and continuously improved. The framework below is organised as a cycle so that development always starts from goals and ends with measured results that feed back into the next cycle.

### 2. The framework

```
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | LAYER 1  STRATEGIC DIRECTION                                              |
 |   Vision, business goals  -->  HRD philosophy and policy  -->  HRD goals  |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | LAYER 2  CAPABILITY REQUIREMENTS                                          |
 |   Competency mapping for each role  -->  Training Needs Assessment        |
 |   (organisation / task / person analysis)  -->  Capability gaps           |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | LAYER 3  INTEGRATED HRD SUBSYSTEMS                                        |
 |   Performance appraisal <-> Feedback and counselling <-> Training and      |
 |   development <-> Potential appraisal <-> Career and succession planning  |
 |   <-> Rewards and recognition <-> Organisation development                |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | LAYER 4  ENABLERS                                                         |
 |   Top-management commitment | Line managers as coaches | HRD department   |
 |   as facilitator | Budget and technology (LMS, HRIS) | OCTAPACE climate   |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
 | LAYER 5  EVALUATION AND RENEWAL                                           |
 |   Kirkpatrick levels 1-4 | HRD climate survey | HRD audit | KPIs -->      |
 |   feedback to Layer 1                                                     |
 +---------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 3. Components explained

| Component | Purpose | Key activities |
|---|---|---|
| HRD philosophy and goals | Ensure alignment | Top management states beliefs about people; HRD goals are derived from business goals (for example "reduce defect rate by 30% through multi-skilling") |
| Competency mapping | Define what capability means | For each role, list the knowledge, skills and attitudes required at "meets" and "exceeds" levels |
| Training Needs Assessment | Find the gaps | Organisation, task and person analysis; performance data; surveys; supervisor inputs |
| Performance appraisal | Set expectations and measure | Joint goal setting (KRAs linked to business goals), self-appraisal, mid-year review, annual review |
| Feedback and counselling | Convert appraisal into development | Structured feedback discussion; individual development plan (IDP) agreed for every employee |
| Training and development | Close the gaps | Blend of on-the-job (coaching, job rotation, projects), off-the-job (workshops, case studies) and e-learning; induction, technical, behavioural and leadership tracks |
| Potential appraisal | Identify future capability | Assessment centres, psychometric tests, potential ratings separate from performance ratings |
| Career and succession planning | Retain and prepare talent | Career paths, talent pools for key positions, fast-track development for high potentials |
| Rewards and recognition | Reinforce learning and performance | Link increments and recognition to performance and to acquired competencies |
| Organisation development | Build culture and teams | Team building, survey feedback, quality circles, participative problem solving |
| Evaluation | Prove and improve | Reaction, learning, behaviour and results for training; annual HRD climate survey; HRD audit; dashboard of KPIs |

### 4. Roles and responsibilities

- **Top management:** approves philosophy, provides budget, reviews HRD dashboard quarterly, models learning behaviour.
- **Line managers:** set goals, coach, give feedback, identify needs, support transfer of learning; development of subordinates is a KRA in their appraisal.
- **HRD department:** designs and integrates the subsystems, facilitates programmes, maintains data and evaluates.
- **Employees:** own their development plan, seek feedback and apply learning.

### 5. Implementation road map

1. **Months 1-3:** Philosophy and policy, competency mapping for critical roles, baseline HRD climate survey.
2. **Months 3-6:** Redesign appraisal with goal alignment; train managers in feedback and coaching; first TNA.
3. **Months 6-12:** Roll out training tracks and e-learning; introduce IDPs; pilot potential appraisal for one level.
4. **Year 2:** Career paths and succession plans for key positions; link rewards; full evaluation cycle.
5. **Ongoing:** annual review of HRD goals against business goals; refresh competency maps as strategy changes.

### 6. Measures of success

Closure of identified skill gaps; improvement in performance KPIs tied to HRD goals; percentage of key positions with ready successors; internal promotion rate; retention of high performers; improved HRD climate scores; training evaluated at behaviour and results levels.

### 7. Conclusion

This basic HRD framework starts from organisational goals, translates them into competency requirements, closes the gaps through an integrated set of subsystems supported by committed leaders and coaching line managers, and closes the loop through evaluation. It improves employee capability precisely because every development effort is anchored in what the organisation is trying to achieve.
